Lesson 3 — Polish It, Then Send It

Part A. Error hunt

Each line has one problem. Find it.

Part B. Peer review

Trade devices with a partner. Open their résumé page and read it. Check only the boxes they really did.

Part C. The email that carries the résumé

Nobody hands a résumé over in silence. It arrives attached to an email. That email is judged too.

To: hiring@laespigabakery.com  |  Subject: Application — Kitchen Assistant Position
Dear Ms. Álvarez,

My name is Elias Ortiz and I am a senior at Galileo Academy. I am writing to apply for the kitchen assistant position posted in your window.

I have worked in a bakery kitchen for two years and I am ServSafe certified. I speak Spanish and English, and I am available after 3:30 p.m. on weekdays and all day Saturday.

My résumé is attached. Thank you for your time, and I hope to hear from you.

Sincerely,
Elias Ortiz
(415) 555-0188

Copy the shape

1. Say your name and your school.
2. Say which job.
3. Say why you fit — two facts.
4. Say when you can work.
5. Say thank you.

Notice

Four short paragraphs. A real subject line. A real name in the greeting — he looked it up. No emoji, no "Hey". And he said his availability, because that is the first thing a manager needs to know.

Notice

The email does not repeat the résumé — it does a different job. The résumé proves competence; the email proves you can be dealt with. Most applicants at this level never send one, so sending a clean four-paragraph email is itself a competitive advantage. The final line is a soft close: it invites a reply without demanding one.

Part D. Say it out loud

A manager will ask: "So, tell me about yourself." You get about 45 seconds. Write it, then practice it with your partner three times.

The shape:
"My name is ___. I'm a ___ grader at Galileo.
Right now I ___ (a job, a team, a responsibility).
I'm good at ___ and ___.
I'm interested in this job because ___."

Eye contact, clear voice, no reading from the screen.
