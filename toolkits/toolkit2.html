TOOLKIT #2

to be · to have · to do

These three verbs show up in almost every English sentence. Be tells what something is. Have tells what something owns. Do makes questions and negatives.

Mr. Toby · ELD
The one-line rule.
to be → I am tall. Use it for what you are like: tall, tired, sixteen, from Yemen.
to have → I have brown eyes. Use it for what you own or carry on your body: eyes, hair, a brother, a phone.
to do → Do you have a pencil? Use it to ask a question or say no with other verbs.

Only he, she, and it get the letter s. He has. She has. It has. Everyone else have. Same trick for every verb in the present tense: he runs, she reads, it works.

3to do

Two jobs. First, do is a real action: I do my homework. Second, do is a helper that builds questions and negatives for other verbs.

WhoYesNoQuestionExampleML

theydo they don'tDo they…?They do not agree.
Helper for a question: Do / Does + person + plain verb. Does she play soccer?  —  not Does she plays
Helper for a negative: person + don't / doesn't + plain verb. He doesn't like loud music.  —  not He doesn't likes
Helper for emphasis: do + plain verb, when someone doubts you. I do know the answer!
Never use do with be.
Ask: Are you tired?   not   Do you are tired?
Say no: She isn't tired.   not   She doesn't be tired.
be makes its own questions and negatives. It does not need help.

4Describe a person

This is the pattern you need for the describing lessons. Notice which verb goes with which word.

She is tall. She has long hair.
He is friendly. He has brown eyes.
They are athletic. They have curly hair.

Eyes and hair are things on your body. You have them. Your height and your personality are what you are like. You are them.
