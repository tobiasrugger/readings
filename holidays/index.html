3Reading A: Where Halloween came from

Read it once without stopping. Then read it again and tap the dotted words.

1More than two thousand years ago, the Celtic people lived in Ireland, Scotland, and northern France. Their year ended on October 31. They called that night Samhain (say it: SAH-win). The Celts believed that on Samhain the door between the world of the living and the world of the dead opened, and spirits could walk on the earth for one night.

2People were afraid, so they did two things. They lit huge bonfires on the hills, and they wore masks and animal skins. If a spirit saw a person in a mask, the spirit would think that person was another ghost and leave them alone. That is the oldest reason for Halloween costumes.

3Later the Catholic Church made November 1 All Saints' Day, a day to honor the holy dead, and November 2 All Souls' Day. The night before All Saints' Day was called All Hallows' Eve. Over hundreds of years, "All Hallows' Eve" wore down into one short word: Halloween.

4In England and Ireland, poor people walked from door to door on those days. They sang or prayed for the family's dead relatives, and the family gave them a small cake called a soul cake. This was called souling. In Scotland, children in costume went from house to house and performed a song, a poem, or a joke to get food or coins. This was called guising. Notice that the children had to do something first. The "trick" in "trick or treat" is what is left of that job.

5In the 1840s a disease destroyed the potato crop in Ireland. More than a million people died and more than a million left. Many came to the United States. They brought Halloween the way every immigrant brings holidays — folded up in the suitcase, next to the clothes.

6They also brought the story of Stingy Jack, a man who tricked the devil twice and then could not enter heaven or hell. He had to walk in the dark forever with one hot coal inside a carved turnip. In Ireland people carved ugly faces into turnips and beets and put them in the window. In America there were pumpkins everywhere, and pumpkins are big and soft and easy to cut. The jack-o'-lantern was born in a new country, like the people who made it.

7Souling and guising slowly turned into trick-or-treating. The words "trick or treat" appeared in American newspapers in the 1920s and 1930s, and after 1945 the custom spread to almost every neighborhood in the country.

8Today Halloween in the United States is not a religious day. It is a night for costumes, candy, decorations, and scary movies, and Americans spend billions of dollars on it. But under the plastic skeletons the old idea is still there. At the end of October, when the nights get long and the leaves die, people think about the dead — and then they laugh at them, so they will not be afraid.

4Reading B: Día de los Muertos

Same job: read once, then read again and tap the dotted words.

1Día de los Muertos means "Day of the Dead." It is not one day. It is November 1 and November 2 — and in many families the work starts weeks before.

2The holiday is a mix of two histories. For thousands of years, Indigenous peoples in Mexico — the Nahua, the Purépecha, the Maya, the Zapotec and others — kept the bones and the memory of their ancestors close, and held a festival for the dead. When the Spanish came in the 1500s, they brought All Saints' Day and All Souls' Day with them. The two traditions did not replace each other. They grew together, like two trees planted in one hole.

3The center of the holiday is the ofrenda, the altar a family builds inside the house. It has levels, like steps. The family puts photographs of the people who died on it, and then the things those people loved: a favorite food, a bottle of soda, a guitar, a baseball, a pack of cigarettes. There is water, because the spirit is thirsty after a long walk. There is salt. There are candles, one for each soul, to show the way.

4And there are flowers — cempasúchil, the orange marigold. Families make a path of orange petals from the street to the altar. The color is loud and the smell is strong, and people say the dead follow it home.

5The food is part of the language of the day: pan de muerto, a sweet bread with bones of dough on top, and sugar skulls with a name written on the forehead — sometimes the name of a living friend, as a joke. Papel picado, cut paper flags, move in the air above the table; people say the movement means the spirits are already in the room.

6November 1 is for the children who died. People call them angelitos, little angels. November 2 is for the adults. Families go to the cemetery, clean the graves, pull the weeds, paint the stone, put down flowers — and then they stay. They eat there. They play music. They tell stories about the dead person, including the embarrassing ones, and they laugh.

7This is the part that surprises visitors: it is not a sad day. The sadness is real, but the holiday is a welcome, not a funeral. Mexicans often say a person dies three times — when the body stops, when the body is buried, and when the last person says their name for the last time. Día de los Muertos exists to push that third death far away.

8It is also not "Mexican Halloween." The dates are close and both have skeletons, but the two holidays came from different places and do different work. One says be afraid and then laugh. The other says come home, we cooked for you.

6The same idea in eight countries

Almost every country on earth has a day for the dead, and many have a day when children get sweets at the door. Read the cards. Then answer the questions under them.

Qingming and the Hungry Ghosts

CHINA

In early April, families go to the cemetery for Qingming — Tomb-Sweeping Day. They sweep the stone, pull the weeds, and set out food, tea, and rice wine. Then they burn paper money and paper houses so the ancestors have what they need on the other side. In the seventh lunar month, the gates open for the Hungry Ghost Festival, and people leave food outside for spirits who have no family left.

Đám giỗ and Vu Lan

VIETNAM

Most Vietnamese homes have a bàn thờ — an ancestor altar with photographs, fruit, and incense. On the death anniversary, đám giỗ, the family cooks the dead person's favorite dishes, invites every relative, and eats together in front of the altar. During Vu Lan, in the seventh lunar month, people burn paper clothes and give rice porridge to wandering souls.

Sat Thai and Phi Ta Khon

THAILAND

In September, families carry food to the temple for their ancestors on Sat Thai, and make krayasat — a sticky sweet of rice, sesame, and peanuts. In the town of Dan Sai, the Phi Ta Khon festival fills the street with young people in tall painted masks made from rice steamers and coconut leaves. They dance and joke as the ghosts.

Gai Jatra and Tihar

NEPAL

At Gai Jatra in August, every family that lost someone during the year walks a cow through the street, because the cow leads the soul. Children dress in costumes, and the whole day is full of comedy and jokes — the joke is the medicine. During Tihar, children go house to house singing Deusi and Bhailo, and every neighbor gives them sweets, fruit, and money.

Eid mornings and Garangao

ARAB AND MUSLIM COUNTRIES

On the morning of Eid, many families go first to the graves to pray for their dead, and then home to eat. Children wear new clothes and collect eidiya — money — and sweets from every adult they visit. In Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman, children go door to door in the middle of Ramadan on Garangao night with cloth bags, singing, and come home full of candy and nuts.

Giant kites for the dead

GUATEMALA

On November 1 in Sumpango and Santiago Sacatepéquez, people spend months building barriletes gigantes — kites of paper and bamboo up to twenty meters across, painted with messages. They fly them in the cemetery, above the graves. The kite carries the message up, and the noise of the paper moves the spirits.

Obon

JAPAN

In August, families clean the family grave and hang lanterns to guide the spirits home. In the town square people dance bon odori in a circle. At the end of Obon, they float paper lanterns down a river — tōrō nagashi — to walk the spirits back out again.

Undás and Pangangaluluwa

PHILIPPINES

On November 1, families come to the cemetery with food, chairs, card games, and sometimes a tent, and stay the whole day and night with their dead. In some provinces an old custom called pangangaluluwa sends children to sing at the door in the voice of the souls, and the neighbors give them food or coins.

7Grammar you need to talk about traditions

A. Simple present — what people do every year

Use the simple present for something that happens every year, not just one time.

I / you / we / they / people / families → visit    he / she / it / my family / the family → visits

People wear costumes.    My grandmother wears black.

1. In Mexico, families (build) an altar in the house.
2. My grandmother (put) flowers on the grave.
3. In China, people (burn) paper money.
4. In Nepal, a family that lost someone (walk) a cow through the street.
5. American children (say) "Trick or treat!"
6. My father (cook) my grandfather's favorite food.
7. In Japan, the family (clean) the grave in August.
8. Marigolds (smell) very strong.
9. In the Philippines, everyone (stay) all night in the cemetery.
10. We (remember) our ancestors.

B. Used to — what you did before you moved

used to + the plain verb = something you did many times in the past, but not now.

In Guatemala I used to fly kites on November 1. Now I go trick-or-treating.

Not: used to flew   Not: used to flying

1. In Vietnam, my family used to (burn) incense every morning.
2. I used to (visit) my grandmother's grave with my mother.
3. We used to (eat) special bread in November.
4. My brother used to (be) afraid of the cemetery.
5. They used to (sing) at every door.
