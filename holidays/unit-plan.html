Ghosts, Candy, and Remembering

Halloween, Día de los Muertos, and how the world talks to the dead

Mr. Toby · Galileo Academy of Science and Technology · ELD newcomers and low beginners · eight days, or three in the short version

What this unit is for

Every student in this room comes from a culture that has a way of speaking to its dead, and most of them have never been asked about it in an American classroom. The unit uses Halloween as the doorway — it is the loudest holiday in the building in October, and it is the one they are most confused by — and then opens out into Día de los Muertos and six more countries, most of which are the countries in this room.

The academic payload is comparison: the language of both, but, while, however, similar to, different from, plus simple present for customs and used to for the life before this one. Those are the structures a newcomer needs in order to say anything true about being an immigrant, and this content gives them something worth saying.

Objectives

  • Read two 200–450 word informational texts at the student's own level and answer literal and inferential questions about them.
  • Compare two holidays in writing using comparison connectors.
  • Use simple present to describe a repeated custom and used to to describe a past habit.
  • Use in, on, and at correctly with months, dates, and times.
  • Explain a tradition from the student's own country to an audience that does not know it.
  • Listen to classmates describe their own traditions and identify what is shared.

Before you start, say this out loud. This unit asks students to name someone who died. Some of them lost people recently. Some lost people on the way here, and some do not know whether a person is alive. Tell the class, in the first five minutes:

• Any question about a dead person can be about anyone — a great-grandparent they never met, a neighbor, a teacher, a pet, a singer, a person from history.

• "I don't want to write about this" is a complete and acceptable answer, and they can tell you privately.

• They never have to read their writing out loud.

Then watch the room on Day 5, during the ofrenda. That is where it usually lands.

The eight days

Day 1 — Whose holiday is it?short version

Students survey their own tradition and see the class data before they read anything.
  1. Do now (5 min). On the board: Draw one thing people do in your country to remember a person who died. No words. Hold them up.
  2. Norms (3 min). Read the note above out loud, in these words.
  3. Part 1 survey (15 min). Students sign in and answer. Circulate — the open question 8 is where you learn who needs a quiet word.
  4. Class results (12 min). Press See the class results, or project the dashboard's Class survey view. Ask: What surprised you? Expect the candy question to surprise them — most rooms find three or four countries with a door-to-door sweets custom nobody knew about.
  5. Part 2 vocabulary (10 min). Set the language selector, walk the table, do the first three cloze items together.
  6. Exit. Finish the ten cloze items.
Differentiation: newcomers do the survey in their own language out loud to a partner first, then write; the checkbox question carries them when writing fails. Stronger students write a second sentence on question 8.

Day 2 — Where Halloween actually came fromshort version

Students read Reading A and correct the assumption that Halloween is American.
  1. Do now (4 min). True or false on the board: Halloween started in the United States. Hands. Do not tell them yet.
  2. First read (8 min). Silent, no stopping, no dictionary. Set the level button first — assign Easier or Full by student, and tell them they may climb.
  3. Second read (12 min). Now tap the dotted words. Partners read a paragraph each out loud.
  4. T/F (5 min). Go back to the do-now question. Who changed their mind?
  5. Questions A1–A6 (15 min). A1 and A2 are literal — everyone. A3 and A4 are the thinking ones. A5 is the one that matters: what did your family carry here?
Differentiation: the Easier text is nine short paragraphs with the same content and the same T/F questions, so the whole class can discuss together without anyone being visibly on the baby version. Sentence frames appear automatically on the Easier level.

Day 3 — Día de los Muertosshort version

Students read Reading B and explain why the holiday is a welcome, not a funeral.
  1. Do now (5 min). Show a photo of an ofrenda with no caption. Write three things you see. Write one question.
  2. First read, second read (18 min). Same routine as Day 2. This is the day the routine pays off.
  3. T/F and B1–B2 (10 min).
  4. Talk (10 min). "A person dies three times." Put it on the board and let them argue. Do not resolve it.
  5. B3–B6 (rest). B5 (whose photo would you put on it?) is the pre-write for Day 5.
Differentiation: if a student stalls on B5, let them choose a public figure. The point is the reasoning, not the relative.

Day 4 — Side by side

Students complete the comparison chart and use comparison connectors in speech before writing them.
  1. Do now (5 min). Two columns on the board. One word each, called out, sorted live: Halloween / Día de los Muertos / both.
  2. Part 5 chart (15 min). Pairs. One student may only look at Reading A, the other only at Reading B. They have to ask each other.
  3. Grammar D, comparison words (12 min). Teach the six connectors, do the six items, check.
  4. Oral practice (8 min). Stand up, find a partner, say one both sentence and one but sentence. New partner. Three rounds.
  5. Write (10 min). The "Mexican Halloween" question, three sentences, using the frame.
Differentiation: the chart is short-answer, so it is reachable for everyone; the connectors turn it into sentences on Day 7. Give the strongest students the "what would a person from the other country not understand?" extension early.

Day 5 — Build an ofrenda

Students build an altar and justify their choices — the emotional and the academic center of the unit.
  1. Do now (5 min). If you could put one object on a table for someone, what object? One word.
  2. Model (6 min). Build one on the screen in front of them. Say why you chose each object, out loud, including the silly one. Your model gives them permission.
  3. Build (15 min). Part 8. Circulate quietly.
  4. Write (12 min). Who it is for, and why two of the objects. This is the day to sit down next to somebody.
  5. Optional, and worth it (rest of period, or a second day). Build a real one on a folding table by the door. Orange paper marigolds, photocopied photos, a paper plate of something. Students add to it all week. Send a note home first.
Differentiation: the object chips mean a student with almost no English can still produce a complete, meaningful artifact. The "something from my country" chip lets them type in their own language if that is what comes.

Day 6 — Eight countriesshort version

Students read eight short texts and find the pattern across all of them.
  1. Do now (4 min). Map on the board. Students put a dot on their country.
  2. Jigsaw (20 min). Groups of four; each student reads two cards and teaches them to the group. Everyone fills in all eight country blanks.
  3. Check (5 min). Press the check button. Fix the misses together.
  4. W1 and W2 (12 min). What is the same in almost every country? Collect answers on the board. You are building the thesis for Day 7 whether they know it or not.
  5. W4 (homework or extension). Write the ninth card about your own country.
Differentiation: assign cards by home country so at least one student in each group is the expert. The W4 card is the multi-level task — three sentences is a pass, and a paragraph is an A.

Day 7 — The grammar that makes it sayable

Students use simple present, used to, and in/on/at accurately in their own sentences.
  1. Do now (5 min). Four sentences on the board, three with errors. Fix them.
  2. Grammar A (10 min). Simple present. The rule box, then the ten items, then check. The -s on my family visits is the one they all miss.
  3. Grammar B (14 min). Used to. Five items, then the two personal sentences. Have six students read theirs out loud — this is the moment the room gets quiet and interested.
  4. Grammar C (8 min). in / on / at. Fast, eight items, check.
  5. Set up the writing (10 min). Read the three levels. Assign a floor, not a ceiling.
Differentiation: Level 1 is six frames to finish, Level 2 is a structured paragraph, Level 3 is two paragraphs with a comparison structure. Tell everyone they may go up a level; the dashboard reports word counts so you see who did.

Day 8 — Write, check, show what you knowshort version

Students produce the writing, self-edit against a checklist, and complete the assessment.
  1. Write (22 min). Quiet. Music if that is your room.
  2. Self-check (6 min). The seven-item checklist in Part 9. Read out loud to yourself is not optional.
  3. Partner read (6 min). Trade. The reader's only job: circle one sentence you liked, and one place you got confused.
  4. Part 10 (12 min). Fourteen questions, then press the button.
  5. Turn in. Everyone presses Turn in before the bell.
Differentiation: the "What can you do now?" self-assessment at the end is not scored and tells you what to reteach. If a class is weak on one row, it is almost always the comparison connectors.

Short version — three days

If October ate your calendar: Day 1 (survey + vocabulary), Day 2+3 combined (both readings, T/F only, questions A5 and B5), Day 6 + writing Level 1 or 2. Skip the grammar sections and the ofrenda, or send the ofrenda home. Part 10 becomes homework.

Level adjustment: score Level 1 writing against the same rubric but read "sentences" for "paragraph." A newcomer who finishes all six frames accurately, with real detail, is a 3 — not a 2. The rubric measures what the student can do with English, not how much of it there is.

Not scored, but read every one

Part 1 question 8, B5, of_who, of_why, and the exit question. These are where you find out which student needs five quiet minutes with you this week.

If you want to extend it

  • Speaking assessment: two minutes, unscripted, "tell me about a holiday in your country" — record it, and compare it to the same prompt in January.
  • Art: sugar-skull line drawings to color, or papel picado cut from folded paper. The folding and cutting is a full listening-and-following-directions lesson on its own.
  • Family interview: three questions home, answers in any language, translated into English in class. This produces the best writing of the year, every year.
  • Real ofrenda by the door, with a card next to it explaining what it is, written by the class, for the rest of the school to read.
  • Cross-check with the survey: after Day 6, return to the Day 1 class charts. Ask what they would answer differently now.
