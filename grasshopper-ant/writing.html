Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper? (easier)

Some people are savers. Some people are spenders.

A spender lives for today. He spends his money fast. He buys a new phone. He eats in restaurants. He buys a nice car. Then the money is gone.

A saver lives with less. She keeps some money in the bank. She keeps it for a bad day.

When I was young, I was a spender. I told myself, "I will save later, when I am older." Then I was older — and I had no money saved.

The ant works in the good times, because he knows the bad times will come. When winter comes, his food is gone from the field. He can only eat what he saved.

Bad things happen when we do not expect them. So it is smart to get ready early. Start now. The winter of your life will come faster than you think.

Adapted for ELD from "Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper?" by Jason White.

Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper? (standard)

This old fable shows the basic difference between savers and spenders. Spenders live for the moment. They spend their earnings on technology, on eating out, and on the payments they have created for nice cars and expensive homes. Savers prefer to live on less and keep the extra money for a hard day later.

The Grasshopper. Most of us have lived the grasshopper's life at some point. When I was young I spent almost everything I earned, and I explained it to myself by saying I would start saving "when I got older." Well, I got older — and I still had no emergency money and almost nothing saved for retirement. I should have been storing my own food away while the finding was easy.

The Ant. Of course the ant is the hard worker. The work ethic of ants is famous. Unlike the grasshopper, the ant works through the good times to prepare for the bad times. He knows that when winter comes, his food in the field will disappear, and he will live on whatever he put away in the warm months.

The moral. When we are young and times are good, it is hard to imagine ever needing savings, or a retirement that is decades away. But bad things arrive when we least expect them, so it makes sense to prepare before they come. Start stockpiling for the winter of your life — it will turn cold before you know it.

Adapted for ELD from "Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper?" by Jason White.

Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper? (challenge)

This old fable shows the basic difference between savers and spenders. Spenders live for the moment, spending their earnings on gadgets, on restaurants, and on the monthly bills they have built for themselves — the nicer car, the bigger apartment. Savers choose to get by on less and put the rest away for a rainy day.

The Grasshopper. At some point, most of us have lived the grasshopper's life. When I was young I spent most of what I earned and justified it by telling myself that I would start saving "when I got older." Well, before I knew it I was older, I still had no emergency fund, and my retirement savings were thin at best. I should have been storing my own kernels away while the getting was good.

The Ant. It is no surprise that the ant is used to describe the harder worker of the two. The work ethic of ants is well documented. Unlike the grasshopper, the ant works through the good times to prepare for the bad times. He knows that when winter arrives his source of food disappears, and he must survive on what he managed to store away in the warmer months. The fable never tells us the fate of the grasshopper — though I suspect the ant would share some of his reserves, assuming the grasshopper had learned his lesson.

The moral. When we are young and times are good, it is hard to imagine living off savings, or needing a retirement that is still decades away. But bad things happen when we least expect them, and it makes sense to prepare for their arrival. That idea sits at the very core of a frugal life. Begin stockpiling for the winter season of your life — it will turn cold before you know it.

Adapted for ELD from "Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper?" by Jason White.

3. Read the other side: "Doing nothing" (everyone)

A writer named Bonnie Tsui read the same fable and disagreed with it.

Tsui remembers the fable this way: the grasshopper played all summer while the ants worked, and when autumn came the ants refused to share. The lesson everyone learns is simple — there is a time for work and a time for play.

But she asks a different question. What if the grasshopper only looked like he wasn't working? He is a musician. Music is his work. Nobody paid him and nobody saw him practice, so nobody counted it.

Tsui argues for what she calls fallow time — time when a field is left empty so that it can grow food again later. People need it too. To do good work, she says, we need to rest, to read, and to reconnect. That quiet time is the invisible work that makes creative work possible.

She admits this is hard. Our culture says that if you are not visibly producing something, you are not worth much. Sitting in a park on a Tuesday looks lazy. People walk by and ask why you don't have a job.

Her answer: “It looks like I’m doing nothing. But it’s the hidden something I’m after.”

Summarized for ELD from Bonnie Tsui, "You Are Doing Something Important When You Aren't Doing Anything," The New York Times, June 21, 2019.
