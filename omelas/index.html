Key vocabulary

Learn these words before reading. You will see them dotted in the story. Tap any dotted word for its meaning.

utopia

A perfect, ideal society where everyone is happy and everything works well

"They dreamed of a utopia where no one was poor."

procession

A long line of people moving slowly together, like in a parade

"The procession of dancers moved through the streets."

paradox

A situation that seems impossible because it contains two opposite truths at once

"The more she slept, the more tired she felt — a paradox."

injustice

When people are treated unfairly and not given their rights

"She spoke out against the injustice of the unfair law."

abundance

A very large amount of something; more than enough

"The market had an abundance of fresh fruit."

neglect

Failing to take care of someone or something that needs care

"The abandoned house showed years of neglect."

degraded

Treated without respect in a way that damages someone's dignity

"No one should be degraded because of who they are."

compassion

A deep feeling of sympathy and care for someone who is suffering

"The nurse showed great compassion for her patients."

Part 1 — The Festival

With a clamor of bells that set the birds soaring, the Festival of Summer came to the city Omelas, bright-towered by the sea☀. The rigging of the boats in harbor sparkled with flags. In the streets between houses with red roofs and painted walls, between old moss-grown gardens and under avenues of trees, past great parks and public buildings, processions moved.

Some were formal: old people in long stiff robes of pale purple and grey, grave master workmen, quiet, merry women carrying their babies and chatting as they walked☀. In other streets the music beat faster, a shimmering of gong and tambourine, and the people went dancing — the procession was a dance.

Children dodged in and out, their high calls rising like swallows crossing in the sky, over the music and the singing. All the processions wound towards the north side of the city, where on the great water-meadow called the Green Fields boys and girls, naked in the bright air, with mud-stained feet and ankles and long, graceful arms, exercised their impatient horses before the race.

The horses wore no gear at all but a halter. Their manes were braided with streamers of silver, gold, and green. They flared their nostrils and pranced and boasted to one another; they were vastly excited, the horse being the only animal who has adopted our ceremonies as his own.

Far off to the north and west the mountains stood up half encircling Omelas on her bay. The air of morning was so clear that the snow still on top of the Eighteen Peaks burned with white-gold fire☀ across the miles of sunlit air, under the dark blue of the sky. There was just enough wind to make the banners that marked the racecourse snap and flutter. In the silence of the broad green meadows one could hear the music winding through the city streets, farther and nearer and ever approaching, a cheerful faint sweetness of the air that from time to time trembled and gathered together and broke out into the great joyous clanging of the bells.

Part 2 — The Narrator Speaks

Joyous! How is one to tell about joy? How describe the citizens of Omelas?

They were not simple folk, you see, though they were happy. But we do not say the words of cheer much any more. All smiles have become ancient.✦ Given a description such as this, one tends to look next for the King, mounted on a splendid horse and surrounded by his noble knights…

But there was no king. They did not use swords, or keep slaves. They were not barbarians.☀ They didn't have monarchy or slavery, and they also got on without the stock exchange, the advertisement, the secret police, and the bomb.

Yet I repeat that these were not simple folk, not gentle shepherds, noble savages, plain utopians. The trouble is that we have a bad habit of considering happiness as something rather stupid.✦ Only pain is intellectual, only evil is interesting. This is the betrayal of the artist: refusing to admit that evil is ordinary, and that pain is terribly boring.

They were not naive and happy children. They were mature, intelligent, passionate adults whose lives were marvelous☀. A miracle! But I wish I could convince you.

Omelas sounds in my words like a city in a fairy tale. Perhaps it would be best if you imagined it as your own perfect place. Happiness is based on deciding what is necessary, what is neither necessary nor destructive, and what is destructive.

But I fear that Omelas so far strikes some of you as goody-goody. Smiles, bells, parades, horses, bleh. So: add a wild celebration if you like, or the faint sweetness of a drug called drooz that brings brilliance to the mind and is not habit-forming✦. For modest tastes, there ought to be beer. A limitless and generous happiness — this is what is felt in the hearts of the people of Omelas.

Part 3 — The Race Begins

Most of the processions have reached the Green Fields by now. A marvelous smell of cooking goes forth from the red and blue tents. The faces of small children are loveable and sticky with pastry.☀ The youths have mounted their horses and are beginning to group around the starting line. An old woman, small, fat, and laughing, is passing out blossoms from a basket, and tall young men wear her flowers in their shining hair.

A child of nine or ten sits at the edge of the crowd, alone, playing on a wooden flute✦. People pause to listen, and they smile, but they do not speak to him, for he never ceases playing and never sees them, his dark eyes wholly rapt in the sweet, thin magic of the tune.

He finishes, and slowly lowers his hands. As if that little private silence were the signal, all at once a trumpet sounds from the pavilion: imperious, sad, piercing.✦

The horses rear on their slender legs, and some of them neigh in answer. Sober-faced, the young riders stroke the horses' necks and soothe them, whispering, "Quiet, quiet, there my beauty, my hope…" They begin to form in rank along the starting line. The Festival of Summer has begun.

Part 4 — The Child

Illustration by Charis Loke — sumi ink, pencil, pastel (2018), made for this story. Used with credit for classroom teaching.

In a basement under one of the beautiful public buildings of Omelas, there is a room. It has one locked door, and no window.✦ A little light seeps in dustily between cracks in the boards. In one corner a couple of mops with stiff, foul-smelling heads stand near a rusty bucket. The floor is dirt, a little damp to the touch. The room is about three paces long and two wide: a mere broom closet.

In the room a child is sitting. It could be a boy or a girl. It looks about six, but actually is nearly ten. It is feeble-minded. Perhaps it was born defective or perhaps it has become this way through fear, malnutrition, and neglect. It picks its nose and fumbles with its toes, hunched in the corner farthest from the bucket and the two mops. It is terrified of the mops.✦ It shuts its eyes, but it knows the mops are still standing there; and the door is locked; and nobody will come.

The door is always locked; and nobody ever comes, except that sometimes the door rattles terribly and opens, and a person, or several people, are there. One of them may come and kick the child to make it stand up. The others never come close, but look in at it with frightened, disgusted eyes. The food bowl and the water jug are hastily filled, the door is locked, the eyes disappear.

"I will be good," it says. "Please let me out. I will be good!"✦ They never answer. The child used to scream for help at night, and cry a good deal, but now it only makes a kind of whining, "eh-haa, eh-haa," and it speaks less and less often. It is so thin there are no calves to its legs; its belly sticks out; it lives on a half-bowl of cornmeal and grease a day. Its body is covered in festered sores.

They all know it is there, all the people of Omelas. Some have come to see it, others are content just to know it is there. They all know that it has to be there. Their happiness, the beauty of their city, the closeness of their friendships, the health of their children, the wisdom of their scholars, even the abundance of their harvest — all of it depends completely on this child's incredible misery.✦

This is usually explained to children when they are between eight and twelve. No matter how well the matter has been explained, this young audience is always shocked and sickened at the sight. They feel disgust, outrage, impotence, despite all the explanations.

They would like to do something for the child. But there is nothing they can do. If the child were brought up into the sunlight, if it were cleaned and fed and comforted, that would be a good thing; but if it were done, all the wealth and beauty and delight of Omelas would wither and be destroyed. Those are the terms.

Often the young people go home in tears, or in a tearless rage. They may brood over it for weeks or years. But as time goes on they begin to realize that even if the child could be released, it would not get much good of its freedom: it is too degraded to know any real joy. Its habits are too socially unacceptable for it to respond to kind treatment.

Their tears at the bitter injustice dry when they begin to understand the terrible justice of reality, and to accept it✦. Yet it is their tears and anger which are perhaps the true source of the splendor of their lives. They know compassion.✦ It is the existence of the child that makes possible the beauty of their architecture, the touching nature of their music, the profundity of their science.

Part 5 — Those Who Walk Away

Now do you believe in them? Are they not more credible? But there is one more thing to tell, and this is quite incredible.

At times one of the young girls or boys who go to see the child does not go home to weep or rage, does not, in fact, go home at all. Sometimes also a man or woman much older falls silent for a day or two, and then leaves home. These people go out into the street, and walk down the street alone. They keep walking, and walk straight out of the city of Omelas, through the beautiful gates.

They keep walking across the farmlands of Omelas. Each one goes alone, boy or girl, man or woman. Night falls; the traveler must pass down village streets, between the houses with yellow-lit windows, and on out into the darkness✦ of the fields. Each alone, they go west or north, towards the mountains. They go on. They leave Omelas, they walk ahead into the darkness, and they do not come back.

The place they go towards is a place even less imaginable to most of us than the city of happiness. I cannot describe it at all. It is possible that it does not exist.✦ But they seem to know where they are going, the ones who walk away from Omelas.

INFER

1 — Why does everyone in the city have to know about the child?

The story tells us that every person in Omelas — adults and children — is told about the child at some point in their life. The narrator explains: "They all know that it has to be there. Some of them understand why, and some do not, but they all understand that their happiness… depend[s] completely on this child's incredible misery." Why is it important that everyone knows? What would happen to the city — or to the people — if they didn't know? Write 2+ sentences using evidence from the story.

4 — If you were raised in Omelas, would you stay or walk away forever?

Most people in Omelas stay. They feel sad and angry when they first learn about the child, but over time they accept it and go on with their happy lives. A few people walk away — forever, into the dark, toward a place that may not exist. If you had grown up in Omelas and knew about the child your whole life, what would you do? Would you stay and enjoy the happiness, knowing what it costs? Or would you walk away, even if it meant leaving everything behind? There is no right answer — but you must explain your reasoning clearly. Write 3+ sentences and be ready to share with the class.

5 — Is Omelas a true utopia? (Discussion question)

A utopia (you-TOE-pee-ah) is a perfect society — a place where everyone is happy, healthy, safe, and free. Omelas seems like a utopia at first: there is no war, no king, no slavery, no poverty. But it depends on the suffering of one child. Can a society truly be "perfect" if one innocent person must suffer to make it work? Is Omelas a utopia, or is it something else? Use at least one detail or quote from the story in your answer.

INFER

6 — Is ignorance bliss? Would the people of Omelas be better off not knowing about the child? (Discussion question)

Ignorance means not knowing something. Bliss means perfect happiness. The expression "ignorance is bliss" means that sometimes it is better — or easier — not to know painful truths. In Omelas, every person is told about the child. They cannot "un-know" it. Think about this: would the people of Omelas be happier if they never found out? What would be gained from not knowing? What would be lost? Think about the narrator's idea that the people's compassion and their sadness is actually part of what makes their lives meaningful. Write 2+ sentences.

7 — Is Omelas a symbol for the real world? (Discussion question)

Le Guin wrote this story in 1973, but many people think it describes something true about the world we live in today. Think about the pre-reading questions: who makes our cheap clothing, our electronics, our coffee and chocolate? Who suffers so that others can be comfortable? If Omelas is a symbol — an image that represents something real — then who is the child in our world? Who are the people of Omelas? And who, if anyone, are "the ones who walk away"? Write 3+ sentences. You can draw on your own knowledge and experience.
