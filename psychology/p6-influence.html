Standard & Challenge

When you make decisions in your life, there are many people and other factors that help you decide. Maybe you choose something because of what your family wants, or maybe you don’t do something because of what your friends want. This is called influence, and it is an important idea in psychology.

Influence is when something affects or changes your mind.

Psychologists divide influences into a few kinds. Family influence comes from parents, grandparents, brothers and sisters, and family traditions. Peer influence comes from people your own age; when it becomes strong it is called peer pressure. Cultureal influence comes from the language you speak, the food you eat, the religion you were raised in, and the values of your society.

Influences are not all the same size. The size or strength of an influence is its magnitude. A song you heard once has small magnitude. A parent’s opinion usually has large magnitude. Part of growing up is noticing which influences you want to keep, and which ones you want to resist.

Two Kinds of Culture

Psychologists describe two broad patterns in how cultures handle the self.

In an independent culture, a person is taught to think of themselves first: What do I want? What are my goals? What makes me special? Choosing your own career, moving away from home at eighteen, and “being yourself” are independent values.

In a collaborative culture, a person is taught to think of the group first: What does my family need? What is my role? What is my obligation? Living with family as an adult, sending money home, and choosing a job your parents respect are collaborative values.

Neither one is better. Most people live somewhere in between — and many students in this room carry both, one at home and one at school. That is not a problem with you. That is two cultures meeting inside one person.

Notice what happens to the ought self in each pattern. In an independent culture, the ought self is quieter, and the ideal self does more of the work — so the common pain is disappointment. In a collaborative culture, the ought self is louder and more crowded with voices — so the common pain is guilt. Same teenager, different weather.
