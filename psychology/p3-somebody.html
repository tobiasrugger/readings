Before You Read

Where and when: East Los Angeles, about 1970. Who: a teenage boy named Chato. He tells the story himself, in his own voice.

The situation: Chato’s neighborhood is being torn down by a railroad company. His gang is gone — some moved, one is in jail, one joined the Navy. He is alone on his street.

1. Chato writes his name on walls all over the city. Before you read: why do you think a person would do that?

2. What does it feel like to be new somewhere, where nobody knows your name?
3. Make a prediction: by the end of the story, will Chato feel more visible or more invisible? Say why now, and check yourself later.

Part 1
My name is Chato. I live in East Los Angeles. Today I quit school. Now I am a writer. I write my name on walls and fences.

“Chato” means Catface. My nose is flat like a cat. It is a Mexican word. I am Mexican. I am not ashamed.

“De Shamrock” is my street and my gang. But my friends are gone now. One is in jail. One joined the Navy. I am the only one left.
This is Chato talking, Chato de Shamrock, from the Eastside in old L.A., and I want you to know this is a big day in my life because today I quit school and went to work as a writer. I write on fences or buildings or anything that comes along. I write my name, not the one I got from my father. I want no part of him. I write Chato, which means Catface, because I have a flat nose like a cat. It’s a Mexican word because that’s what I am, a Mexican, and I’m not ashamed of it. I like that language, too, man. It’s way better than English to say what you feel. After Chato I write “de Shamrock.” That’s the street where I live, and it’s the name of the gang I belong to, but the others are all gone now. Their families had to move away, except Gorilla is in jail and Blackie joined the Navy because he liked swimming. In the good old days nobody messed with us. But now I’m the only one left.

Part 2
This morning my father shouted, “It is 6:30!” I shouted back, “I am quitting school!”

My father asked in Spanish, “Don’t you want to be a lawyer and help Mexican people?”

I said, “I am leaving! You will never see me again!”

Then I stayed in bed. I woke up at 11:00. I put on clean jeans and combed my hair very neat. I felt that today was a big day.
Well, today started off like any other day. My father coughs and spits about nineteen times and hollers it’s six-thirty. So I holler back I’m quitting school. “Don’t you want to be a lawyer no more,” he says in Spanish, “and defend the Mexican people?” “I’m checking out of this dump! You’ll never see me again!” I laid in bed and waited for him to go off to work. When I woke up again, it was way past eleven. I can sleep forever these days. So I got out of bed and put on clean jeans and my windbreaker and combed myself very neat because already I had a feeling this was going to be a big day for me.

Part 3
My street is broken now. Before, there were good houses on both sides. Then the railroad company bought all the land. Only my father’s house is left.

The machines came. They ripped down the houses. It sounded like the houses were screaming.

At my front gate I saw a mark. It is the sign of the Sierra Street gang. I rubbed it off. Tonight they will put it back.

Before, they were afraid of us. But without your gang, you are nobody.
Every time I go out my front door I have to cry for what they’ve done to old Shamrock Street. It used to be so fine, with solid homes on both sides. Then the S.P. railroad brought up all the land except my father’s place. They came in with their wrecking bars and their bulldozers. You could hear those houses scream when they ripped them down. I hope that never happens to your street, man. My first stop was the front gate and there was that sign again, the big S wrapped around a cross like a snake with rays coming out, which is the mark of the Sierra Street gang. I rubbed it off, but tonight they’ll put it back again. In the old days they wouldn’t dare to come on our street, but without your gang you’re nobody.

Part 4
I walked down Main Street like a ghost. To prove I am alive, I wrote my name on a fence.

Other people write sloppy. Not me. I write slowly and carefully.

I took crayons and chalk from the store. I wrote my name all down Main Street in eight colors.

Then I had an idea. When you write your name on something, it belongs to you. So I wrote my name on the market, the liquor store, the barber shop, the furniture store, and the firehouse. Now they are mine.
So I cruised on down to Main Street like a ghost in a graveyard. Just to prove I’m alive, I wrote my name on the fence at the corner. A lot of names you see in public places are written very sloppy. Not me. I take my time. I went into the dime store and helped myself to two boxes of crayons and some chalk and cruised on down Main, writing all the way. I left a fine little trail of Chato de Shamrock in eight colors. On one wall I also drew a little picture that the girls would be interested in and put down a phone number beside it. I bet a lot of them are going to call that number, but it isn’t mine because we don’t have a phone in the first place, and in the second place I’m probably never going home again. I continued my travels till something hit me. You know how you put your name on something and that proves it belongs to you? Things like school books or gym shoes? So I put my name on the Triple A Market and on Morrie’s Liquor Store and on the Zocalo. I took over a barber shop and a furniture store and the firehouse.

Part 5
Then the Sierra gang came down the street. Eight or ten boys. I hid in a garage. I am not a coward. They did not see me.

I went to the Boy’s Club. It is safe there. I played basketball and pool. The TV show was boring, so I wrote my name on the TV screen.

Mr. Calderon made me clean it off. He said, “It is hard to lose your gang. But you can make new friends here. Why not come to school?”

I said, “The Sierra will get me.”

He said, “The Sierra forgot about you. Maybe you put that mark on your house, to feel big.”

I said, “That was my name! I like to write my name!”

He said, “So do dogs. On every lamppost.”
Along came the Sierra right then, eight or ten of them down the street with that stupid walk. I ducked into a garage. Not that I’m a coward. The Sierra didn’t see me and went on by. I couldn’t hear what they were saying but I knew they had me on their mind. So I cut on over to the Boy’s Club, where they don’t let anybody get you, no matter who you are. I shot some baskets and played a little pool and watched the television, but the story was boring, so it came to me, Why not write my name on the screen? Up comes Mr. Calderon and makes me wipe it off. And he takes me into his office and closes the door. “I know it’s hard to lose your gang, Chato,” he says, “but this is your chance to make new friends and straighten yourself out. Why don’t you start coming to the Boy’s Club more?” “It’s boring here,” I tell him. “What about school?” “I can’t go,” I said. “They’ll get me.” “The Sierra’s forgotten you’re alive,” he tells me. “Then how come they put their mark on my house every night?” “Maybe you put that mark up yourself,” he says. “To make yourself big. Just like you wrote on the television.” “That was my name! I like to write my name!” “So do dogs,” he says. “On every lamppost they come to.”

Part 6
It was getting dark. I saw my name everywhere.

At the Zocalo I saw a big red heart around my name. Someone drew it with lipstick.

I found the person at the telephone company. It was Rabbit. We call her Rabbit because of her big teeth.

She said, “You write your name so nicely. Let’s go writing together!”

I almost said yes. It would be nice to have a friend. But I remembered my reputation.

I said, “Go away, Rabbit. I don’t want a partner.”

She got angry and called me ugly. She spit at me. I threw away her lipstick.
Out on the street it was getting dark, but I could still see Chato written everyplace. At the Zocalo I stopped. Around my name there was a big red heart done in lipstick. What guy carries lipstick? And if it was a girl, that could be kind of interesting. I caught up with her at the telephone company. There she is, standing in the shadows, drawing her heart around my name. And then she turns around and it’s only Rabbit. That’s what we called her growing up, because of her big teeth. When she sees me, she takes off down the alley, but I catch her and get the lipstick away from her very easy. “What right you got to my name?” I tell her. “I never gave you permission.” “You sign yourself real fine,” she says. “Let’s go writing together.” “The Sierra’s after me.” “I don’t care,” she says. “Come on, Chato — you and me can have a lot of fun.” I’m ashamed to say it but I almost told her yes. It would be a change to go writing with a girl. We could talk there in the dark. But then I remembered I had my reputation to think of. “Run along, Rabbit,” I told her. “I don’t want no partners.” “Who are you calling Rabbit?” she screamed. “You ugly, squash-nose punk.” She spit at my face but missed. I left and threw away her lipstick.

Part 7
I went to the banks. Many people walk there. I wrote my best work ever. Under the street light my name looked like gold.

I watched the people. A rich man saw my name and smiled. I think he would give me a job.

A girl stopped and looked at my name for a long time. I think she wanted to know me. I almost said hi. But I didn’t.

Now my chalk is gone. But I had a vision. I don’t need to be a movie star. I only need chalk. I will be famous in the whole city. I will be like a ghost — mysterious. People will only know my name.
Then I went over to the banks, which is a good spot for writing because a lot of people pass by there. That was the best work I’ve ever done in all my life. Under the street lamp my name shone like solid gold. I stood to one side and checked the people as they walked past. There was one man. He got out of his Cadillac to buy a paper and when he saw my name he smiled. I bet he’d give me a job if I asked him. Later a girl came by. She stopped and really studied my name like she fell in love with it. She wanted to know me, I could tell. I almost said “Hi” to that girl, and, “How do you like my writing?” But not quite. So here I am, standing on this corner with my chalk all gone. But I just had a vision, man. I don’t need to be a movie star or boxing champ to make my name in the world. All I need is plenty of chalk. And that’s easy. I’ll be famous all over town. Of course they’ll try to stop me, but I’ll be like a ghost, man. I’ll be real mysterious, and then all they’ll know is just my name, signed like I always sign it, CHATO DE SHAMROCK with rays shooting out like from the Holy Cross.

Think Deeper

1. Chato says, “without your gang you’re nobody.” What does he mean?
He means that ______ . Without a group, a person feels ______ .
2. The title is “The Somebody.” Who is the somebody? Is Chato a somebody or a nobody? Explain.
I think Chato is a ______ because ______ .
3. Mr. Calderon compares Chato to a dog marking lampposts. Chato does not like this. Is Mr. Calderon right or is he cruel? Say both sides.
Mr. Calderon is right because ______ . But he is unfair because ______ .
4. Chato almost says yes to Rabbit, and almost says hi to the girl at the bank. He does neither. What stops him both times? What does that tell you about him?
5. Chato’s reputation is the thing he protects most — more than his safety, more than friendship. Trace the moments in the story where reputation costs him something. Then argue: is his reputation real, or is he the only person who believes in it?
6. The story is told by Chato himself. What might a reader see about Chato that Chato cannot see about himself?
