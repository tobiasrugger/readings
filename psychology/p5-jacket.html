Part 1
My clothes have failed me.

I remember a green jacket. I wore it in fifth and sixth grade.

I needed a new jacket. My mother asked, “What kind do you want?” I said: black leather, silver studs, many belts — like the bikers wear. She listened for a long time while she cooked. I thought she understood.

The next day I came home. On my bed was a jacket. It was the color of old guacamole. Green and ugly.

I walked to it slowly, like it was a stranger. I wanted to cry. The jacket was ugly and too big. I knew I had to wear it for a long time. I was small and thin. A new jacket would take years.
My clothes have failed me. I remember the green jacket that I wore in fifth and sixth grade. When I needed a new jacket and my mother asked what kind I wanted, I described something like bikers wear: black leather and silver studs, with enough belts to hold down a small town. We were in the kitchen, steam on the windows from her cooking. She listened so long while stirring dinner that I thought she understood for sure the kind I wanted. The next day when I got home from school, I found on my bed a jacket the color of day-old guacamole. I threw my books on the bed and approached the jacket slowly, as if it were a stranger whose hand I had to shake. I wanted to cry because the jacket was so ugly and so big that I knew I’d have to wear it a long time. I was a small kid, thin as a young tree, and it would be years before I’d have a new one. I stared at the jacket, like an enemy, thinking bad things before I took off my old jacket, whose sleeves climbed halfway to my elbow.

Part 2
I put the jacket on. I zipped it up and down. I rolled the cuffs so they did not cover my hands. I put my hands in the pockets and flapped the jacket like a bird.

I looked in the mirror. I brushed my hair. I looked ugly.

I walked past my mother in the kitchen. I smiled and said thank you.

In the backyard I kicked a ball. I climbed the fence. I played with my dog. The dog bit me and made a hole in the sleeve.

Then I sat in my bedroom, in the dark, with the jacket on my lap.
I put the big jacket on. I zipped it up and down several times and rolled the cuffs up so they didn’t cover my hands. I put my hands in the pockets and flapped the jacket like a bird’s wings. I stood in front of the mirror and brushed my hair to see what I would look like doing something natural. I looked ugly. Finally I went out to the backyard, smiling a “thank you” to my mom as I passed her in the kitchen. With my hands in my pockets I kicked a ball against the fence, and then climbed it to sit looking into the alley. I played with my dog until he bit me, tearing a hole in the sleeve of the jacket. Then I went to my bedroom to sit with my jacket on my lap, with the lights out.

Part 3
The next day I wore the jacket to school.

I got a D on a math quiz.

A bully pushed me to the ground. He told me to stay there.

The girls turned away and whispered.

Even the teachers looked at me and said I looked foolish.

At lunch it was cold, but I took the jacket off. I played kickball in a thin shirt. My arms were cold.

After lunch I put the jacket back on and shivered until I was warm. Everybody saw me. Nobody said “that’s ugly” out loud. But I heard the gossip and the laughing. I knew it was about me.
That was the first afternoon with my new jacket. The next day I wore it to school and got a D on a math quiz. In the morning a bully pushed me to the ground and told me to stay there. The girls turned away to whisper to each other. The teachers were no help: they looked my way and talked about how foolish I looked in my new jacket. Even though it was cold, I took off the jacket during lunch and played kickball in a thin shirt, my arms feeling cold. But when I returned to class I slipped the jacket on and shivered until I was warm. Everybody saw me. Although they didn’t say out loud, “Man, that’s ugly,” I heard the buzz-buzz of gossip and even laughter that I knew was meant for me.

Part 4
I wore that jacket for three years. The sleeves got short. My arms stuck out like turtle necks.

In those three years, no girl liked me. At lunch I sat with the ugly boys by the fence.

I blame the jacket for those bad years. I blame my mother for her bad taste and her cheap ways. It was a sad time for my heart.

I spent sixth grade in a tree in the alley with one friend. I was waiting for something good to happen.

Then I started to grow. My chest got strong. The hole from the dog got bigger. The stuffing fell out. The elbows cracked.

I showed my mother the cracks. She said, “Children in Mexico would love that jacket.”

I said, “This is America!” I yelled that my sister Debbie did not have a jacket like mine. I ran outside and climbed a tree, almost crying.

Then my mother called me for dinner. We ate beans and tortillas. After dinner I went outside. The sky was cold. I put on my jacket again — that green ugly brother, always breathing over my shoulder.
I wore that thing for three years until the sleeves grew short and my arms stuck out like the necks of turtles. All during that time no love came to me — no little dark girl in a Sunday dress she wore on Monday. At lunchtime I stayed with the ugly boys who leaned against the fence. I blame that jacket for those bad years. I blame my mother for her bad taste and her cheap ways. It was a sad time for the heart. With a friend I spent my sixth-grade year in a tree in the alley, waiting for something good to happen to me in that jacket, which had become the ugly brother who tagged along wherever I went. And it was about that time that I began to grow. My chest puffed up with muscle, and my hands hardened for the coming fights. The rip from when my dog bit me got bigger; I taped it up but the stuffing still fell out. That winter the elbows began to crack. I showed the cracks to my mother, and she said that there were children in Mexico who would love that jacket. I told her that this was America and yelled that Debbie, my sister, didn’t have a jacket like mine. I ran outside, ready to cry, and climbed a tree. I was called to dinner: my mother said grace, and my brother and sister made ugly faces at their glasses of milk. I eagerly ate big rips of buttered tortilla that held scooped-up beans. Finished, I went outside with my jacket across my arm. It was a cold sky. I climbed the fence, started up the alley, and soon slipped into my jacket, that green ugly brother who breathed over my shoulder that day and ever since.

Challenge Questions

7. The boy thinks everyone is laughing at him. How is this similar to Chato in “The Somebody”?
8. In Part 4, the mother says “there were children in Mexico who would love that jacket.” Explain what she means in your own words.
9. Imagine you are talking to the boy and his mother about the jacket. Write two questions you could ask each of them.
10. The boy blames the jacket for three bad years. Reread Part 3 carefully. How much of his misery is actually caused by the jacket, and how much is caused by what he believes people are thinking? Use two quotes.
11. An adult is telling this story about his childhood. Find one place where the adult voice is judging the child, and one place where it still feels the child’s pain. What is the difference between them?
12. Chato writes his name everywhere so people will see him. The boy takes his jacket off at lunch so people will not see him. Both boys are managing how they are seen. Which strategy costs more? Defend your answer with evidence from both stories.
