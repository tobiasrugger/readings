Easier

Psychology is the study of people. It studies the mind. It studies behavior — the things people do.

Psychologists ask questions like these:

Why do people love?
Why do people hate?
Why are we nice to some people and mean to other people?
Why do some people lead and other people follow?

Psychologists do research and experiments. Then they write a theory. A theory is a big idea that explains many things.

This year we will study one part of psychology. It is called social psychology. Social psychology studies people with other people: in families, with friends, in class, in a crowd.

Social psychology asks three big questions. They are our questions for this unit:

Who am I?
How do I influence others?
How do others influence me?

Standard & Challenge

Psychology is the study of the human mind and human behavior. Research in psychology tries to understand and explain how people think, act, and feel. Why do people fall in love? Why do people hate each other? Why are we nice to some people and mean to other people? Why do some people lead while other people follow? Why do we think some people are beautiful while we think other people are not?

Psychologists do research and experiments to learn the answers to these and many other questions about how the human mind works. Based on their research, they develop scientific theory — big ideas that help explain almost everything about our human world.

In our English class this year, we will focus on one area of psychology called social psychology. Social psychology studies individuals and their relationships to other people. It studies how people behave in families, with friends, with classmates, with people of the same race and with people of a different race, with lovers, in a crowd, in a small group.

Three of the most important questions social psychology asks are: Who am I? How do I influence others? How do others influence me?

Challenge — extra

Social psychologists are especially interested in adolescence, the years between childhood and adulthood. During adolescence, the brain changes fast. So does identity — your sense of who you are. Teenagers begin to ask, for the first time and on purpose: Am I the person my family says I am? Am I the person my friends say I am? Am I someone else?

This is also when empathy grows. Younger children mostly notice what they themselves want. Adolescents become much better at holding two minds at once: their own, and someone else’s. That skill is exactly what a reader needs. When you read a story and ask “why did he do that?”, you are doing social psychology.
