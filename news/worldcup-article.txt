TITLE: The Best Player Lost

---READ:A---
On July 19, Spain played Argentina in the World Cup final. The game was in New Jersey. More than 80,000 people were there. Millions more watched at home.

For 90 minutes, nobody scored. Spain attacked again and again. Argentina defended. In [[goal|the place where you try to put the ball]] for Argentina was Emiliano Martínez. He [[stopped|did not let something continue]] shot after shot. He made more saves than any goalkeeper in the history of a World Cup final.

Then Argentina lost a player. Enzo Fernández got a second yellow card. Argentina had to play the rest of the game with ten men.

In the 106th minute, Ferran Torres scored for Spain. Torres was not even a [[starter|a player who is on the field when the game starts]]. He waited. He [[came on|entered the game]] in the 62nd minute. Spain won, 1-0.

{{It was Spain's second World Cup. Spain gave up only one goal in the whole tournament.}}

But many people did not talk about Spain after the game. They talked about Martínez. {{He played the best game of his life, and he lost.}}

---READ:B---
On July 19, Spain played Argentina in the final of the World Cup. The game was at MetLife Stadium in New Jersey, in front of 80,663 people, and millions more around the world watched at home. It was the last of 104 matches, and the first time North America had hosted the men's tournament in more than thirty years.

For ninety minutes, nobody scored. Spain attacked and attacked — twenty shots by the end of the match, against Argentina's two. Argentina [[defended|worked to stop the other team from scoring]]. And in [[goal|the place where you try to put the ball]] stood Emiliano Martínez, who stopped everything. He saved a free kick from Lamine Yamal that was curling toward the corner. He saved shot after shot after shot. By the end he had made more saves than any goalkeeper in the history of a World Cup final.

Then Argentina lost a man. In [[stoppage time|extra minutes added at the end]] at the end of the second half, Enzo Fernández fouled a Spanish defender and received his second yellow card. He walked off. Argentina played all of extra time with ten players against eleven.

In the 106th minute it finally broke. A ball bounced loose in the box, and Ferran Torres — who was not a [[starter|a player who is on the field when the game starts; the other players wait and come on later]], who [[came on|entered the game]] in the 62nd minute — hit it with his left foot, just under the [[crossbar|the bar across the top of the goal]]. Spain won, 1-0.

{{It was Spain's second World Cup, after 2010. They gave up only one goal in the entire tournament, which no champion had ever done before.}}

And yet when the game ended, a great many people were not talking about Spain. {{They were talking about the goalkeeper who played the best match of his life and lost it anyway.}}

---READ:C---
On the nineteenth of July, in front of 80,663 people at MetLife Stadium in New Jersey, Spain met Argentina in the final of the World Cup. It was the last of 104 matches, the close of the first men's tournament North America had hosted in more than three decades, and by most accounts it was not a beautiful game. It was, however, a revealing one.

For ninety minutes nobody scored, and not for lack of trying. Spain finished the match with twenty attempts on goal; Argentina managed two. Spain held the ball for roughly sixty percent of the game. What kept the score at zero was partly an Argentine defense that threw itself in front of everything, and partly Emiliano Martínez, who spent the afternoon in [[goal|the place where you try to put the ball]] doing something close to the impossible. He pushed away a free kick from Lamine Yamal that was curling toward the far post. He stopped shots from distance and shots from close range. By the final whistle he had recorded more saves than any goalkeeper in the history of a World Cup final.

Then the match turned on a single [[reckless|not careful; dangerous]] moment. Three minutes into stoppage time at the end of the second half, Enzo Fernández caught a Spanish defender with a foul bad enough that the referee produced the card immediately — his second of the match. Argentina would play the entirety of extra time a man short.

The goal, when it came, came in the 106th minute, and it came from a [[substitute|a player who does not start the game, and comes on later to replace someone]]. Ferran Torres had entered in the 62nd, meaning he had spent the first hour of the biggest match of his life sitting down, watching. A ball bounced loose inside the box, and Torres struck it left-footed just beneath the [[crossbar|the bar across the top of the goal]]. Afterward he said the goal belonged to forty-seven million people, which is the population of Spain.

{{Spain had won its second World Cup, sixteen years after its first. It had conceded exactly one goal across the whole tournament — something no previous champion had ever managed.}}

And yet a remarkable number of people left that stadium, and turned off their televisions, talking about somebody on the losing side. {{Martínez played the finest match of his career and lost it, which is a thing that happens, and which no amount of effort protects you from.}}

---GRAMMAR---
NOTE: For most verbs in the past, add <b>-ed</b>: <i>play &rarr; played</i>, <i>watch &rarr; watched</i>, <i>defend &rarr; defended</i>.
NOTE: But the most common verbs in English are <b>irregular</b>. They change completely, and you have to learn each one.
NOTE: <b>win &rarr; won</b> &nbsp; <b>score</b> is regular, but <b>get &rarr; got</b> &nbsp; <b>make &rarr; made</b> &nbsp; <b>stand &rarr; stood</b> &nbsp; <b>come &rarr; came</b> &nbsp; <b>hit &rarr; hit</b> (no change at all).
NOTE: In the negative, the past goes on <i>did</i>, not on the main verb: <b>Argentina did not score.</b> Never <i>did not scored</i>.
