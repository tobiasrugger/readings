---READ:A---
It is 7:30 in the morning. You are in class. Your eyes are heavy. The teacher is talking. You are not lazy. Your body is doing exactly what a teenager's body is built to do.

Here is the science. A teenager needs ((more sleep than)) an adult. Doctors say nine hours a night. Most teenagers get ((less than)) eight. In one big survey, seven out of ten high school students said they do not get enough sleep on school nights.

But the real problem is not how much. It is when. When you become a teenager, your body clock changes. Your body makes its sleep [[chemical|a substance your body makes]] about two hours ((later than)) it used to. So at 10 p.m., an adult is sleepy. A teenager is wide awake. {{A teenager's body wants to sleep at 11 and wake up at 8. School starts at 7:30.}}

Some places are changing this. In 2022, California made a [[law|a rule made by the government]]. High schools cannot start ((earlier than)) 8:30 in the morning. In Seattle, one school district moved its start time almost an hour later. Students slept about half an hour more each night. Their grades went up.

What about us? Galileo's first bell rings at 8:40. That is ten minutes later than the law says it must. It is not an accident. People [[argued|gave reasons and fought with words]] for years to make it happen. But think about it: if your body wants to wake up at 8, and you leave home at 7:45, you are still waking up ((earlier than)) your body wants.

Not everyone likes this. Buses cost more. Sports practice ends late. Some parents have to be at work early.

{{But the science is clear: the problem is not the teenagers. It is the clock.}}

---READ:B---
It is 7:30 in the morning and you are in first period. Your eyes are heavy, the teacher's voice is far away, and you are fighting to stay awake. You might think you are lazy. You are not. Your body is doing exactly what a teenager's body is built to do.

Start with how much sleep you need. Doctors say a teenager needs between eight and ten hours a night — ((more than)) an adult needs, and far more than most teenagers get. In a large national [[survey|a set of questions asked to many people]], seven out of ten high school students said they get ((less than)) eight hours on school nights. Many get six.

But the bigger problem is not how much you sleep. It is when. Around age thirteen, your [[body clock|the system inside you that controls when you feel sleepy and awake]] shifts. Your brain starts releasing melatonin, the [[chemical|a substance your body makes]] that makes you sleepy, about two hours ((later than)) it did when you were ten. So at 10 p.m., your parents are yawning and you are wide awake. {{A teenager's body wants to fall asleep around 11 and wake up around 8. Most high schools start before 8.}}

That is why some scientists call early school start times a kind of [[jet lag|the tired feeling after flying to a different time zone]] that happens every single week.

Some places have listened. In 2022, California passed a [[law|a rule made by the government]] saying public high schools cannot start ((earlier than)) 8:30 a.m. — the first state to do it. Before that, Seattle moved its high school start time nearly an hour later and then measured what happened. Students slept about 34 minutes more per night. Grades went up. Fewer students were absent or late.

Which brings us to Galileo. Our first bell rings at 8:40 — ten minutes later than California requires, and almost an hour later than the 7:50 bell that was normal in American high schools for decades. That is not an accident. It is the result of parents, doctors, and students [[arguing|giving reasons for an idea and defending it]] for years, and winning. But look at the numbers again. If your body wants to wake at 8, and you are on the bus at 7:45, you are still waking ((earlier than)) your body is built for. {{The 8:40 bell did not solve the problem. It made it smaller.}}

Not everyone is happy about it. Later start times mean later [[dismissal|the time school ends]], which pushes sports practice into the dark. Buses that serve both elementary and high schools have to run on a tighter schedule, which costs money. And parents who start work at 7 cannot always wait for an 8:30 bell.

{{But the science is not really in dispute anymore. The problem was never the teenagers. It was the clock.}}

---READ:C---
It is 7:30 in the morning and you are in first period. Your eyelids are heavy, the teacher's voice seems to arrive from a great distance, and you are negotiating with yourself over whether to put your head down for just a moment. The conclusion most adults draw from this scene — that you are lazy, or stayed up too late on your phone — is almost certainly the wrong one. Your body is doing precisely what an adolescent body is designed to do.

Consider first the quantity. Pediatricians recommend that teenagers sleep between eight and ten hours a night, which is ((more than)) adults require and considerably more than most teenagers manage. In a national [[survey|a set of questions asked to many people]], roughly seven in ten high school students reported sleeping ((fewer than)) eight hours on school nights, and a substantial share reported six or less.

The quantity, though, is downstream of a timing problem. Beginning in early adolescence, the [[body clock|the system inside you that controls when you feel sleepy and awake]] shifts later. The brain begins releasing melatonin — the [[chemical|a substance your body makes]] signaling that it is time to sleep — roughly two hours ((later than)) it did in childhood, and the shift is biological rather than behavioral. At 10 p.m. an adult is yawning; a fifteen-year-old is, physiologically, nowhere near ready. {{The adolescent body is built to fall asleep near 11 and wake near 8. Most American high schools ring their first bell before 8.}}

Some researchers describe the result as chronic social [[jet lag|the tired feeling after flying to a different time zone]]: a body kept permanently on one schedule by a society that insists on another. The consequences show up in the data as lower grades, higher rates of depression and anxiety, more car accidents among teenage drivers, and more caffeine.

A few places have acted on the evidence. In 2022 California became the first state to [[mandate|to order by law]] that public high schools begin no ((earlier than)) 8:30 a.m. Several years before that, Seattle's public schools shifted their high school start time by nearly an hour and allowed researchers to measure the effect. Students gained a median of 34 minutes of sleep per night, grades rose, and attendance improved, particularly at the lower-income school in the study.

Which brings the question home. Galileo's first bell rings at 8:40, ten minutes past the legal minimum and nearly an hour later than the 7:50 start that was standard in American high schools for most of the last century. It is worth being clear that this did not happen by itself; it is the product of a long [[campaign|an organized effort to change something]] by pediatricians, parents, and students, and of a legislature that eventually listened. It is also worth being clear about what it did not do. A body that wants to wake at 8 and a student who boards the 7:45 bus are still roughly an hour apart. {{The 8:40 bell did not close the gap between adolescent biology and the school day. It narrowed it, which is a different and more modest achievement.}}

The objections are practical rather than scientific, and they are real. A later start means a later [[dismissal|the time school ends]], pushing practices and games toward darkness. Districts that run one bus fleet for elementary and secondary schools lose the staggered schedule that makes that possible, and the fix is expensive. Parents who report to work at 7 cannot simply wait for an 8:30 bell.

{{What has quietly changed is that nobody serious disputes the biology anymore. The argument is now only about logistics, which is a different kind of argument — and the teenager asleep in first period was never the problem.}}
