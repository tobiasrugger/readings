---READ:B---
Almost every snake in the world moves the same way: it pushes its body from side to side across the ground. This is called [[slithering|moving like a snake, with the body bending side to side along the ground]]. But scientists ((have now studied)) a tiny snake that does something no one expected. When it is in danger, it turns into a wheel and rolls away.

The animal is the dwarf reed snake. It lives in Southeast Asia, hiding under dead leaves on the forest floor, and it comes out mostly at night. It is very small, usually shorter than a school ruler, and many animals want to eat it, including birds and bigger snakes.

People in Southeast Asia ((have known)) about the rolling for a long time, but scientists only recorded it officially in 2023. This year a team led by Yosuke Kojima, at Toho University in Japan, wanted to understand exactly how the snakes do it. They built a small [[track|a long, narrow path for racing]], placed the snakes at one end, and tapped them gently. Then they filmed what happened with fast cameras from several angles.

The snakes curled into a loop and rolled. Their fastest speed was almost four meters per second — about four times faster than they can slither, and faster than many people can run.

{{The team has found one more surprise. While the body spins around and around, the snake keeps its head steady, so it can still see where it is going.}}

Kojima's team ((has shown)) that rolling is a fast way to escape, and it may also [[confuse|make someone unsure about what is happening]] a hungry bird. {{Now some scientists wonder if every snake has the body to roll — and most of them have simply never tried.}}

---READ:C---
For as long as there have been snakes, there has been one basic way to be a snake: you press your long body against the ground and push yourself forward in a series of side-to-side waves. Scientists call it lateral undulation; most people call it [[slithering|moving with the body bending side to side along the ground]]. Now researchers ((have taken)) a close look at a small group of snakes that, at the worst possible moment, abandon slithering altogether and turn themselves into wheels.

The dwarf reed snakes of Southeast Asia are easy to overlook. They live in the leaf litter of the forest floor on islands like Borneo, they come out mostly at night, and even a fully grown one is shorter than a school ruler. For birds and larger snakes, that makes them a convenient snack — which is exactly why their escape trick matters.

Local people ((have long known)) that these snakes roll, and the behavior was officially recorded for the first time in 2023. What nobody understood was how. So a team led by Yosuke Kojima of Toho University in Japan built a miniature racing [[track|a long, narrow path designed for racing]], placed the snakes at one end one at a time, tapped them gently, and filmed the result from several angles with high-speed cameras. Their findings were published this fall in the journal Current Biology.

When threatened, the snakes arched into a loop and tumbled forward, pushing off the ground with the tail at the end of each turn. At their fastest they reached 3.69 meters per second — roughly four times their top slithering speed, and quicker than many people can sprint.

{{The most surprising detail is that the head barely moves. While the rest of the body cartwheels around it, the snake holds its head steady, the way a bird keeps its head level while its body bobs and sways.}} Researchers suspect this lets the snake keep watching for danger, and the sudden spinning may also [[confuse|leave someone unsure of what is happening]] a predator that was expecting an ordinary slithering meal.

The study raises a strange question. The dwarf reed snake's body is not very different from the bodies of thousands of other snakes. {{So some scientists have begun to wonder whether many snakes have everything they need to roll, and have simply never used it.}}

NOTE: The <b>present perfect</b> is <b>have</b> or <b>has</b> + the past participle: <i>Scientists <b>have found</b> something new.</i>
NOTE: News uses it all the time. Something happened in the past, but it is important <b>now</b>, and we do not say exactly when.
NOTE: <b>he / she / it / the team / one name</b> → <b>has</b>: <i>The team <b>has shown</b>…</i> &nbsp; <b>I / you / we / they / scientists</b> → <b>have</b>.
NOTE: If you say <b>when</b> (yesterday, last week, in 2023), use the past simple instead: <i>Scientists <b>recorded</b> it in 2023.</i> Never <i>have recorded in 2023</i>.
