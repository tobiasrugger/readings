Do spiders sleep? Do they dream? Scientists in Germany ((have studied)) this question. They ((have found)) something surprising.

The scientists studied a small [[jumping spider|a spider that jumps to catch food; it does not wait in a web]] from Australia. It is about the size of a coin. It hunts other spiders. It is very smart.

The scientists put each spider in a small plastic [[dish|a flat, round container]]. They watched the spiders at night with special cameras. The cameras can see in the dark.

The spiders hung upside down and slept. Sometimes their eyes moved fast. Their legs [[twitched|moved suddenly and quickly, without trying]]. People do this too when we dream.

{{The scientists have learned that spiders have two kinds of sleep. One kind is like human dream sleep.}}

---READ:B---
Do spiders sleep? And if they sleep, do they dream? Scientists at the University of Bonn in Germany ((have spent)) many days and nights trying to answer these questions, and they ((have found)) something that surprised them.

The scientists studied a [[jumping spider|a spider that jumps to catch food instead of waiting in a web]] from Australia called Portia. It is about the size of a coin, and it is a clever hunter. It eats other spiders. To catch them, it shakes their web like a trapped insect, and then it attacks.

The team put each spider alone in a small plastic [[dish|a flat, round container]], twelve centimeters wide. They filmed the spiders at night with [[infrared|a kind of light that people cannot see; special cameras use it to see in the dark]] cameras, so they did not need to turn on a light.

At night the spiders hung upside down from a thread and went still. Then, at times, their eyes moved quickly and their legs [[twitched|moved suddenly and quickly, without trying]]. In people, this kind of sleep is called REM sleep. It is the sleep in which we have our strongest dreams.

The scientists also needed to show that the spiders were really asleep, not just resting. So they played sounds and checked how slowly the spiders reacted. They kept some spiders awake, too. Later, those tired spiders slept more, just like a tired person does.

{{The team has shown that these spiders have two different stages of sleep, and one of them looks a lot like human dream sleep.}}

So do spiders dream? The scientists say they do not know yet. {{They could not look inside the spiders' brains, so they have only seen what the spiders do, not what they feel.}}

---READ:C---
Every night, a small brown spider in an Australian forest lets itself down on a thread, turns upside down, and goes still. For a long time nobody knew what was happening inside it. Now scientists at the University of Bonn in Germany ((have watched)) that stillness closely, frame by frame, and they ((have found)) that it is far more complicated than it looks.

The spider is Portia fimbriata, a [[jumping spider|a spider that hunts by stalking and leaping, rather than waiting in a web]] about the size of a coin. Portia is unusually clever for an animal so small. It hunts other spiders, and one of its tricks is to pluck their webs so that the vibrations feel like an insect caught in the silk. When the owner of the web comes to collect its meal, Portia is waiting.

To study its sleep, the team placed each spider alone in a plastic [[dish|a flat, round container]] twelve centimeters across and recorded it for several days and nights. They used [[infrared|light that human eyes cannot see, which special cameras use to film in darkness]] cameras so they would never have to switch on a light and disturb the animals.

What they saw was not one kind of rest but two. In the first, the spider simply hung quietly. In the second, its eyes moved rapidly and the muscles of its legs [[twitched|jerked in small, sudden movements that the animal does not control]]. In humans, a stage with exactly those signs is called REM sleep — rapid eye movement — and it is the stage in which we have our most vivid dreams.

Stillness alone does not prove sleep, so the researchers went further. They played sounds and measured how long the spiders took to respond. They also kept some spiders awake and found that the spiders made up for it afterward by sleeping more — what scientists call "sleep rebound." {{Together, these tests have given the first full experimental proof that spiders really sleep, in distinct stages, rather than just sitting still.}}

That leaves the question in the title. The researchers are careful here. They could not measure the spiders' brain activity, only their behavior, and eye movements are not the same thing as dreams. {{Whether a spider dreams — and what a spider would even dream about — is a question science has not yet been able to answer.}}

NOTE: The <b>present perfect</b> is <b>have</b> or <b>has</b> + the past participle: <i>Scientists <b>have found</b> something new.</i>
NOTE: News uses it all the time. Something happened in the past, but it is important <b>now</b>, and we do not say exactly when.
NOTE: <b>he / she / it / the team / one name</b> → <b>has</b>: <i>The team <b>has shown</b>…</i> &nbsp; <b>I / you / we / they / scientists</b> → <b>have</b>.
NOTE: If you say <b>when</b> (yesterday, last week, in 2023), use the past simple instead: <i>They <b>published</b> the study in October.</i> Never <i>have published in October</i>.
