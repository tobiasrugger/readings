TITLE: Every Dish Is an Immigrant
DEK: When people move, their food moves with them. Then it changes.
WEEK: 2026-09-21
DEFAULT: B
GRAMMAR: used to — how things were before, but are not now
SOURCE: Written for our class by Mr. Toby, after reading about how we eat now in The New Yorker.

---READ:A---
Look at a banh mi. It is a Vietnamese sandwich. But the bread is French. Why? France [[ruled|controlled; was the government of]] Vietnam for a long time. The French brought their bread. Later, the French left. The bread stayed. Vietnamese cooks kept the bread. They put in carrot, cilantro, chili, and pork. They made something new.

The same thing happens everywhere. Pizza started in Naples, Italy. Poor workers ((used to eat)) it in the street. Rich people did not eat it. Then Italians moved to New York. They brought pizza with them. Now people eat pizza in almost every country.

Look at San Francisco. The big burrito in [[foil|thin silver metal paper]] is from here. It started in the Mission in the 1960s. In Mexico, a burrito is small. It has no rice.

Some people do not like this. They say the food is not [[real|true, not fake]] anymore. But food always moves. Italy ((used to have)) no tomatoes. The tomato came from Mexico. Thai food ((did not use to be)) spicy. Chili peppers came from America.

So, is a dish from one place only? Or is it from every person who cooks it?

---READ:B---
Think about a banh mi. Most people would say it is a Vietnamese sandwich, and they are right. But look at the bread. That is a French baguette. For almost a hundred years, France [[ruled|controlled; was the government of]] Vietnam, and the French brought their bread with them. When they left, the bread stayed. Vietnamese cooks kept the baguette, threw away the rest of the French sandwich, and filled it with [[pickled|kept in vinegar or salt water so it tastes sour]] carrot, cilantro, chili, and pork. They made something new.

This happens everywhere. People in Naples, Italy ((used to eat)) pizza standing up in the street. It was cheap food for poor workers, and rich people would not touch it. Then Italians moved to New York and brought it with them. Today you can buy pizza with corn and mayonnaise in Japan and pizza with sweet potato in Korea.

You do not have to go far to see this. The giant burrito wrapped in [[foil|thin silver metal paper]] — the one you can buy on almost every block in the Mission — is a San Francisco [[invention|a new thing that someone made for the first time]]. It started here in the 1960s. In most of Mexico, a burrito is small, and there is no rice inside it.

Some people do not like this. They say that when a dish moves, it loses something [[real|true, not fake]], and that a burrito with rice is not really a burrito at all. Other people [[argue|say why you think you are right]] that food has always [[traveled|moved from place to place]]. Five hundred years ago, Italy ((used to have)) no tomatoes at all. The tomato came from Mexico. Italian cooks ((did not use to make)) tomato sauce, because no Italian had ever seen a tomato. Chili peppers came from the Americas too. Food in Thailand, Korea, and India ((did not use to be)) spicy.

So which is it? Does a dish [[belong|be owned by; come from]] to one place only? Or does it belong to whoever cooks it?

Maybe the honest answer is that both things are true. A dish carries a place inside it. And a dish changes in the hands of every person who cooks it, forever.

---READ:C---
Consider the banh mi. Nobody would hesitate to call it Vietnamese, and nobody would be wrong. But the bread is a French baguette, and it is there for a reason that has nothing to do with cooking. France [[ruled|controlled; was the government of]] Vietnam for nearly a century, and [[colonial|belonging to a country that controls another country]] officials wanted the bread they had eaten at home. When the French finally left, the bread did not leave with them. Vietnamese cooks kept the baguette, discarded everything else about the French sandwich, and filled it instead with [[pickled|kept in vinegar or salt water so it tastes sour]] carrot, cilantro, chili, and pork. What they made was not French, and it was not what Vietnamese food had been a century earlier. It was something genuinely new.

That pattern repeats almost everywhere you look. In Naples, people ((used to eat)) pizza standing up in the street, folded in half, bought for almost nothing. It was working people's food, and wealthier Italians wanted no part of it. Then southern Italians emigrated to New York by the hundreds of thousands and carried it with them, where it grew larger, cheaper, and eventually respectable. Today a pizza in Japan may arrive topped with corn and mayonnaise, and one in Korea with sweet potato, and neither is a mistake.

You need not travel to find an example. The enormous foil-wrapped burrito sold on nearly every block of the Mission is not Mexican in any simple sense. It is a San Francisco [[invention|a new thing that someone made for the first time]] dating to the 1960s. Across most of Mexico a burrito is modest in size and contains no rice whatsoever.

Not everyone welcomes this. There is a familiar [[argument|the reasons a person gives for their idea]] that a dish loses something essential when it crosses a border, that a burrito stuffed with rice has stopped being a burrito, and that calling it one is a kind of forgetting. The counterargument is harder to dismiss than it first appears. Food has never stayed still. Five hundred years ago Italy ((used to have)) no tomatoes at all. The tomato arrived from Mexico, and Italian cooks ((did not use to make)) tomato sauce for the simple reason that no Italian had ever laid eyes on a tomato. Chili peppers likewise crossed from the Americas, which means the food of Thailand, Korea, and much of India ((did not use to be)) spicy. The dishes we now call [[traditional|the way something has been done for a very long time]] are frequently younger than the word suggests.

So which is it? Does a dish [[belong|be owned by; come from]] to one place, or to everyone who has ever cooked it?

The most honest answer is probably that both are true at once. A dish carries a place inside it, and it is remade by every pair of hands it passes through. Which is also, more or less, a description of the people who cook.

---GRAMMAR---
NOTE: Use <b>used to</b> for something that was true before, but is not true now.
NOTE: <b>Italy used to have no tomatoes.</b> (Before: no tomatoes. Now: many tomatoes.)
NOTE: The negative is <b>did not use to</b> — with no <i>d</i> on <i>use</i>.
NOTE: <b>Thai food did not use to be spicy.</b> (Not: <i>did not used to</i>.)
NOTE: After <b>used to</b>, the verb is plain: <i>used to eat</i>, <i>used to live</i>. Never <i>used to ate</i>.
BANK: used to, did not use to
