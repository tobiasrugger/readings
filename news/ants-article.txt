66 million years ago, a giant rock from space hit the Earth. It was an [[asteroid|a big rock that flies through space]]. Almost all the dinosaurs died.

But ants did not die. After the asteroid, there were more and more kinds of ants. Today there are more than 15,000 kinds of ants in the world.

Why did ants do so well? Scientists in Germany ((have studied)) this question. They ((have looked)) at the [[DNA|the instructions inside every living thing; they tell the body how to grow]] of 163 kinds of ants.

Some pieces of DNA can move. They are called [[jumping genes|small pieces of DNA that can move or copy themselves to a new place]]. The scientists ((have found)) that ants with many jumping genes have many kinds of ants in their family.

{{The jumping genes became very busy soon after the asteroid. Then many new kinds of ants appeared.}}

---READ:B---
About 66 million years ago, a huge [[asteroid|a big rock flying through space]] crashed into the Earth near what is now Mexico. Dust filled the sky and blocked the sun. Plants died, and then the animals that ate them. Almost all the dinosaurs disappeared.

Ants were already alive back then, but there were not many kinds. After the crash, everything changed. Ants spread across the world and split into thousands of new [[species|kinds of animals or plants]]. Today scientists know more than 15,000 kinds of ants.

Why did ants do so well when so many other animals died? A team led by Lukas Schrader at the University of Münster in Germany ((has tried)) to answer that question. They compared the [[DNA|the instructions inside every living thing that tell its body how to grow]] of 163 different ant species.

They looked closely at something called [[jumping genes|small pieces of DNA that can move or copy themselves to a new place]]. Jumping genes can change the DNA around them, and sometimes those changes help an animal in a new world. The team ((has found)) that the ant families with the most jumping genes also have the most species today.

{{The jumping genes in many ant families became very active soon after the asteroid hit — and after that, those families grew into many new species.}} The scientists also connected jumping genes to the genes ants use to smell. Ants find food, find their way home, and recognize their own family almost completely by smell.

Still, the scientists are careful about what they say. {{They have shown that jumping genes and new ant species came at the same time. They have not proved that one caused the other.}}

---READ:C---
About 66 million years ago, an [[asteroid|a large rock traveling through space]] roughly the size of a city slammed into the Earth near what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico. The dust it threw into the sky blocked the sun for months. Plants died, then the animals that ate the plants, then the animals that ate those animals. Except for the ancestors of today's birds, the dinosaurs were gone.

For ants, the same catastrophe turned out to be an opening. Ants had already existed for tens of millions of years, but they were not especially common. After the impact they spread across the planet and split again and again into new [[species|distinct kinds of animals or plants]]. Scientists ((have now described)) more than 15,000 species of ants, and they are found on every continent except Antarctica.

Why did ants thrive in a broken world? A research team led by Lukas Schrader of the University of Münster in Germany ((has offered)) a new answer. They compared the [[DNA|the chemical instructions inside every cell that tell a living thing how to grow and work]] of 163 ant species, covering most of the major branches of the ant family tree, and published their findings this fall in the journal Science Advances.

Their focus was a strange kind of DNA known as [[jumping genes|stretches of DNA that can move or copy themselves to new places in the genetic code]]. When a jumping gene lands in a new spot, it can change how nearby genes work. Usually that does nothing, and sometimes it does harm — but now and then it produces a change that helps. The team found that the ant lineages carrying the most jumping genes are the ones with the most species today.

{{More striking was the timing: in the ancestors of several of the largest ant groups, jumping-gene activity surged shortly after the asteroid hit, before those groups exploded into new species.}} The researchers also linked jumping genes to the growth of the gene families ants use to smell — and smell is how ants do almost everything, from following a trail to recognizing a nestmate.

Here, though, the scientists choose their words carefully, and so should we. {{The study has shown that jumping genes and new species go together and that the genes came first. It has not proved that the genes caused the explosion of ants — and in science, those are two very different claims.}}

---GRAMMAR---
NOTE: The <b>present perfect</b> is <b>have</b> or <b>has</b> + the past participle: <i>Scientists <b>have found</b> something new.</i>
NOTE: News uses it all the time. Something happened in the past, but it is important <b>now</b>, and we do not say exactly when.
NOTE: <b>he / she / it / the team / one name</b> → <b>has</b>: <i>The team <b>has shown</b>…</i> &nbsp; <b>I / you / we / they / scientists</b> → <b>have</b>.
NOTE: If you say <b>when</b> (66 million years ago, yesterday, in October), use the past simple instead: <i>The asteroid <b>hit</b> the Earth 66 million years ago.</i> Never <i>has hit 66 million years ago</i>.
NOTE: Useful participles: find &rarr; <b>found</b> &nbsp; show &rarr; <b>shown</b> &nbsp; study &rarr; <b>studied</b> &nbsp; look &rarr; <b>looked</b> &nbsp; prove &rarr; <b>proved</b> &nbsp; try &rarr; <b>tried</b> &nbsp; be &rarr; <b>been</b>
