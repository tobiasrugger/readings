Short Stories & Fables

ELD 1 · Newcomers and low beginners · Galileo Academy · Mr. Toby

Four weeks · 20 instructional days · five texts

A story is the same everywhere. Can you find its parts in English?

What this unit does

Newcomers arrive already knowing how stories work. Most of them know a version of the ant and the grasshopper, or the tortoise and the hare, in their own language. That prior knowledge is the whole point: students are not learning what a fable is, they are learning to name its parts in English and to talk and write about it in the past tense. Comprehension is nearly free, so all the load can go on language.

The five texts get longer and harder in order. Every text runs through the same eight steps, so by text three the routine disappears and students think about the story instead of the worksheet.

By the end, students can

  • Name and find the five story elements: characters, setting, conflict, plot, resolution — plus the moral.
  • Retell a story in order using first, then, next, after that, finally.
  • Use the simple past, including 25+ irregular verbs, in speech and writing.
  • Write a paragraph of 5–7 sentences giving an opinion about a story, with a reason.
  • Read 50 new high-frequency words and use them in a sentence.

How a text runs — the eight steps

Every text uses the same eight steps in the student app. Roughly two to four days per text.

Daily sequence

Week 1 — Story elements, and text 1
DayLesson
1What is a story? Students tell a story from their own country in their own language to a partner, then in any English they have. Introduce characters and setting only. Drive Flash cards & posters folder.
2Elements with no reading load. Watch a silent clip — Chaplin, or the Lucy chocolate factory. Students name characters, setting, problem, ending. Add conflict, plot, resolution, moral. Build the anchor chart you will keep on the wall for four weeks.
3Ant and Grasshopper: Before → Words → Read. Talk Page Experts read-aloud in groups: each student owns one paragraph and reads it to the group.
4Ant and Grasshopper: Order → Elements. Print Cut-up sentence strips as the hands-on version of Order for students who need to move pieces.
5Grammar: regular past. Add -ed. Ten verbs, five rewrites. Then the quiz. Check the dashboard before you go home.
Week 2 — Text 2, and the irregular past
DayLesson
6Tortoise and Hare: Before → Words → Read. Watch a version of the race on video first, then read.
7Order → Elements. Then compare: the ant and the tortoise both win. How? Talk Both ___ and ___ win because they ___.
8Grammar: irregular past. Toolkit #10 out on every desk. run→ran, sleep→slept, wake→woke, win→won. Print The Rabbit/Turtle past tense sheet from the Drive folder is the paper twin of this step.
9Write: Why does the tortoise win? Frames on the board. Five sentences minimum, seven for anyone who finished early last week.
10Group quiz (student-led protocol, below), then the app quiz.
Week 3 — Text 3, and the argument
DayLesson
11Boy Who Cried Wolf: Before. Spend real time on the three agree/disagree statements — a small lie is not a big problem will divide the room. Then Words → Read.
12Order → Elements. Listen-and-repeat video for pronunciation and intonation of the shouted lines.
13Grammar: said, asked, shouted, cried. Quotation marks, capital at the start, punctuation inside. Ten verbs, five rewrites.
14Write: Whose fault is it — the boy's or the villagers'? This is the first question in the unit with no safe answer. Take both sides seriously.
15Compare: The Boy Who Cried Bigfoot next to the original. Same moral? Different? Then the quiz.
Week 4 — Two more texts, and the assessment
DayLesson
16Lion and Mouse — all eight steps in one day. Students are fast by now; let them feel it. Short text, familiar routine, quick win.
17Gift of the Magi: Before → Words → Read paragraphs 1–6. Set the scene: New York, 1905, $1.87 is about $60 today.
18Read to the end → Order → Elements. The moment to teach: both of them sold the thing the other one's present was for. Ask what word we have for that.
19Write: Was it worth it? Then quiz.
20Unit assessment (below).

Three levels, one classroom

Everyone does the same eight steps. What changes is the text, the support, and how much they produce.

Tier 1 — Newcomer, limited literacy (arrived this year)
  • Easier English text only. Use the ▶ buttons — listen first, then read along, then read alone.
  • Translate language set in the green bar; tap every gold word.
  • Order step: give them the printed cut-up strips instead of the screen.
  • Elements: copy the sentence frame and fill one blank. One sentence per box is a full answer.
  • Grammar: first five verbs only.
  • Write: three sentences using the frame word for word.
  • Quiz orally with their group leader if reading it alone stalls them.
Tier 2 — Low beginner (the middle of the room)
  • Easier English for texts 1–3, then try Full story for texts 4 and 5.
  • All eight steps, all ten verbs.
  • Elements: two sentences per box, frames available but not required by text 3.
  • Write: 5–7 sentences with a reason and one detail from the story.
Tier 3 — High beginner / long-term EL
  • Full story from day one. Compare the two levels and name what changed.
  • Write 8–10 sentences and quote one line from the text: One line that supports my idea is, "___."
  • Extension reading: Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper? — the savers-and-spenders article in the Drive folder. Vocabulary table, then a paragraph on which one they are.
  • Cross-text task: pick two of the five and argue which moral is more useful.
Grouping that works: mixed-tier groups of four for the read-aloud and the group quiz, same-tier pairs for writing. The Tier 3 student running the group quiz gets more language practice than anyone.

Assessment

Daily — the dashboard

Eight ticks per student per story at readings/fables/teacher.html, password galileo2026. It shows which level they read, sequence score, verb score, words written, quiz percent, and whether they turned it in. Sort by quiz score to find your reteach group in about four seconds. CSV export for the gradebook.

Weekly — the group quiz

Student-led, on whiteboards, in mixed groups of four. One student is the leader and has the script; the rest write. Keep your existing protocol — it is the best speaking-and-listening assessment in the unit because the leader has to give instructions, check work, and correct politely.

  1. Spelling from dictation. Leader says a word; group writes it. Five words plus two full sentences from this week's story.
  2. Capitals and alphabet. Leader spells a word letter by letter without saying it. Group writes it, then circles the capital and the period.
  3. Describing pictures. Leader shows an image from the story; each student writes one complete sentence. Check for capital, verb, period.
  4. Elements out loud. What does "setting" mean? What is the setting in this story?

Leaders get the script the day before. Translate the opening instructions into the languages in the room.

End of unit — Day 20

Students pick one of the five stories and work alone, no frames on the wall:

  1. Complete a blank Story Elements page for that story from memory. Print
  2. Retell the plot in six sentences using first, then, next, after that, finally — all in the past tense.
  3. Write a paragraph: what is the moral, and do you agree with it? 5–7 sentences, one reason, one detail from the story.
  4. Twenty-word spelling and meaning check drawn from the fifty unit words.

Going further

Nonfiction pair — after text 1

Are You an Ant or a Grasshopper? Savers and spenders. Vocabulary table in home language, then a paragraph with a quotation. Good for Tier 3, or for the whole class as a read-aloud.

The counter-argument — after text 1

The Tsui piece arguing that doing nothing is doing something important. Too hard to read whole at this level; pull three sentences and ask whether the grasshopper was right after all.

Fables from home

Every student writes a fable from their own country in the same eight-step shape. Best culminating task in the unit; publish them as a class book.

Perform it

Groups of four act out one fable with a narrator. Scripts come straight from the dialogue in the Full story version.
