The words (Latin, and what they mean)

"Ave Maria" is a very old Catholic prayer. Latin is the language of the old Roman Church. Ave means hail — a greeting for someone you honour.

Ave Maria, gratia plena,
Dominus tecum.
Benedicta tu in mulieribus,
et benedictus fructus ventris tui, Iesus.

Sancta Maria, Mater Dei,
ora pro nobis peccatoribus,
nunc et in hora mortis nostrae.
Amen.
Hail Mary, full of grace,
the Lord is with you.
Blessed are you among women,
and blessed is the child you will have, Jesus.

Holy Mary, Mother of God,
pray for us sinners,
now and at the hour of our death.
Amen.

A note for the curious: Schubert did not set these Latin words. He wrote a German song about a girl named Ellen who prays to Mary. Singers later swapped in the Latin prayer, and that version is the one the world remembers. Even the famous "Ave Maria" is already a borrowing.

Who was Cesária Évora?

Stromae did not invent the title "Ave Cesaria." He borrowed the shape of a prayer and put a different woman inside it.

Cesária Évora (1941–2011) was a singer from Cape Verde, a group of ten small islands in the Atlantic Ocean, off the west coast of Africa. Cape Verde is a country of people who leave: for generations, islanders sailed away to find work and did not come back.

She sang morna — slow, sad island music. Her great subject was sodade.

She often sang barefoot on stage, and people called her the Barefoot Diva. She said she went barefoot for the poor women and children of her country. She was not famous outside Cape Verde until she was about fifty. She died in 2011. Stromae released "Ave Cesaria" two years later.

Stromae's own background matters here too: his father was Rwandan, his mother Belgian, and his father was killed in the 1994 genocide. Songs about people who are gone are not a new subject for him.

Ave Maria → Ave Cesaria

The word for this

An allusion is when an artist points at something famous without explaining it. They borrow its power. If you recognise it, the work means more. If you don't, you miss a layer.

Stromae only had to change one word. Ave Maria → Ave Cesaria. Everything you already felt about the first title now sits on top of the second one.
