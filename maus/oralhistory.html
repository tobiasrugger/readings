Oral History — Final Project

Find one person. Ask the questions nobody asked. Publish it.

not saved yet
Vladek only told his story because Art kept asking. It took him years and he was not always nice about it. Now it is your turn: find one person and ask them the questions nobody has asked. At the end, your story goes into a real book that your family can hold.

0. Where am I?

Tap a step when you finish it. Mr. Toby sees this, so he knows who needs help without asking you in front of everyone.

1. Who will you interview?

It does not have to be a famous person or a sad story. It has to be someone whose life you do not fully know. A parent, a grandparent, an uncle, a neighbour, a coach, a person at your job, someone at your place of worship. It can be someone who does not speak English. You will be the translator — that is a skill most people in this building do not have.

Their name
Who are they to you?
What language will the interview be in?
What do you want to know about them? Write one thing. What is the one thing you most want to know about this person? What is the question you are slightly afraid to ask? Write it here first.
I want to know about ______.
Before you ask: tell them what the project is, tell them it will be published in a class book, and ask if that is okay. If they say some part is private, that part stays out. If they get upset, you stop. Those are the rules real oral historians follow.

2. Open or closed?

A closed question gets you one word. An open question gets you a story. You want stories. Sort these — they are checked as you go.

1Did you like your school?
2What was your school like?
3How old were you when you left?
4Tell me about the day you left.
5Were you scared?
6What were you most afraid of?
7Do you miss it?
8What do you miss most, and why?
9Was it hard?
10What was the hardest part for you?
11Can you describe the room you slept in?
Change this closed question into an open one: “Was the journey difficult?”
Open questions often start with: What… How… Tell me about… Can you describe… Why…

    4. Practise first

    Before you use these on a real person, run three of them on someone at your table. Knee to knee. Eye contact. Do not write while they talk — just listen, then write.

    5. Record the interview

    Record on your phone, or record right here. Recording in your home language is correct — do not make the person speak English for you. You translate later.

    0:00

    No phone, no recording, no problem — write the answers by hand during the interview and type them below. Many real oral historians work that way.

    6. Write down what they said

    Listen a little, pause, write. Do not translate yet. Write it in the language they spoke.

    7. Put it into English

    You are the translator. It does not have to be perfect English. It has to sound like them. Keep the way they say things.

    Where is it thin? Find two places where you want more. Write them here, then go back and ask again.

    8. Turn it into a story

    An interview is a list. A story has a person, a place, a problem, and a change. Pick one moment from the whole interview — not the whole life — and tell that.

    Where and when
    In ______, in the year ______, ______.
    The problem
    One day, ______.
    What changed
    After that, ______.

    Add what a camera would see

    Spiegelman does not write “it was cold.” He draws snow on a coat. Give us three things we could see, hear or smell in this moment.

    One simile or metaphor about this person
    Simile: ______ was like ______.   Metaphor: ______ was ______.

    9. Choose how you publish it

    Three ways to finish. All three are worth the same. Choose the one that shows your work best.

    If you chose comic: plan your panels. One box = one moment. Write what we SEE in each.

    10. Edit before you publish

    Read your story out loud to a partner. Then check each box. Your partner checks too.

    11. Look back

    What is one thing you learned about this person that you did not know? What surprised you most in this interview? Art Spiegelman says he never really understood his father. Did this project change how you see your person? How?
    Vladek’s story survives because someone wrote it down. What happens to a story nobody writes down?
