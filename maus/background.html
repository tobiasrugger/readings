2. How to read a comic everyone can do this

Maus is a graphic novel — a real book told in pictures. This is good news: you can understand a lot of this book before you can read all of the words. Here is the grammar of comics. It takes ten minutes to learn and it works in every language.

Panels and the gutter

A is one box. You read them left to right, then down — like English text. The white space between panels is the . The gutter is where time passes. The artist does not draw what happened in between. Your brain fills it in. That is the trick of every comic ever made.

VVisualizerMaus I p. 33

Four kinds of balloon

If the tail is a point, the character is speaking out loud. If the tail is bubbles, it is a private thought — nobody else hears it. A box in the corner is the narrator. In Maus, the boxes are usually Vladek telling the story in 1978.

Open to any page of Maus. Find one of each and write the page number.

Why are the people animals?

Spiegelman draws Jews as mice, Germans as cats, Poles as pigs, Americans as dogs, French as frogs, Swedes as reindeer.

He did not invent this. Nazi newspapers called Jews rats and vermin. Spiegelman takes the insult and makes the reader look straight at it for two hundred pages. A cat chasing a mouse is not a fair fight — that is the point.

But watch what happens when the metaphor breaks: Art’s wife Françoise is French and Jewish, and Art cannot decide what animal to draw. In Maus II he draws people wearing masks. Keep a note every time the animal idea stops working.

3. Timeline: how did this happen?

  • 1918 Germany loses World War I. The country is poor and angry.
  • 1933 Hitler takes power. The first camp, Dachau, opens in March.
  • 1935 Nuremberg Laws: Jews are no longer German citizens.
  • 1938 Kristallnacht. Synagogues burned across Germany and Austria.
  • 1939 Germany invades Poland. Vladek is called to the army.
  • 1940 Ghettos are sealed in Polish cities.
  • 1941 Germany invades the USSR. Mass shootings begin.
  • 1942 Death camps begin operating. Most of the murders happen in 1942.
  • 1944 Vladek and Anja are taken to Auschwitz.
  • 1945 Death marches. Liberation. Roughly six million Jews have been murdered, along with Roma, disabled people, Polish civilians, Soviet prisoners, and gay men.
  • 1948 The UN adopts the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
  • 1986 Maus I is published. Maus II follows in 1991.

4. Ten stages — genocide is a process, not an event

Genocide never starts with killing. It starts with words. Genocide Watch describes ten stages that repeat across history — Armenia, Cambodia, Rwanda, Bosnia, Darfur, Myanmar. The early stages are happening somewhere right now. Every stage can be stopped.

1Classification

People are split into “us” and “them.”

In Maus: Jews are defined as a separate race, not as Germans.

2Symbolisation

The group is given a name or a mark.

In Maus: The yellow star. In Maus, Jews are drawn as mice.

3Discrimination

The group loses rights by law.

In Maus: Jews cannot own businesses, go to school, or hold jobs.

4Dehumanisation

The group is talked about as animals, disease, vermin.

In Maus: Nazi posters called Jews rats. Spiegelman takes that insult and makes it the whole book.

5Organisation

Special groups are trained and armed.

In Maus: The SS, the camps, the trains, the schedules.

6Polarisation

Moderates are silenced. Mixing is forbidden.

In Maus: Poles who help Jews are executed — see Mrs. Motonowa’s fear.

7Preparation

Lists, registration, ghettos, transport.

In Maus: The stadium selection. The registration at Dienst.

8Persecution

Property taken, people moved, starvation begins.

In Maus: Srodula. The bunkers. The coupons.

9Extermination

Mass killing.

In Maus: Auschwitz-Birkenau, 1942–1945.

10Denial

It is hidden, and then people say it never happened.

In Maus: Why Spiegelman puts a technical diagram in a comic book.

5. Who was murdered?

Six million Jews — two out of every three Jews in Europe. Also murdered: about 1.8 million non-Jewish Polish civilians, up to 500,000 Roma and Sinti, around 250,000 disabled people, 3.3 million Soviet prisoners of war, and thousands of gay men, Jehovah’s Witnesses and political prisoners.

Numbers that big stop meaning anything. So Maus tells you about one family: Vladek, Anja, and a little boy named Richieu who does not survive the book.
