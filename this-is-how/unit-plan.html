Unit plan

This Is How We Do It & This Is How I Do It

Matt Lamothe · ELD 101/102 newcomers and low beginners · Galileo Academy · Mr. Toby

Two picture books, one structure. Each spread asks the same question of every child on Earth — where do you live, who do you live with, how do you play, how do you help — and answers it in pictures. For newcomers that repeated frame is the whole point: the same sentence pattern comes back eight times with new content, so students read for information on day one and produce language by day eight. The unit ends with students writing and illustrating their own page for the book.

What students will be able to do

  • Find specific information in an illustrated text and answer in a complete sentence.
  • Describe a picture in 3–5 sentences using I see, There is, There are.
  • Compare two texts and two lives using both, also, but, while, and -er than.
  • Use the unit grammar accurately: superlatives, there is/are, prepositions of place, possessives, third-person -s, simple past, and transportation phrases.
  • Write and illustrate an eight-panel autobiographical page modeled on the books.

Standards touched: CA ELD Part I.6 (reading closely), I.11 (supporting opinions), I.12 (selecting language resources), Part II.1 (understanding text structure), II.3 (using verbs and verb phrases), II.4 (using nouns and noun phrases), II.5 (modifying to add details). Scale the same task across Emerging, Expanding, and Bridging using the built-in level switch.

Materials

  • Class set or shared copies of both books (photocopies of the key spreads work for the "how I help" cross-text questions).
  • Student app: tobiasrugger.github.io/readings/this-is-how/ — all eight lessons, the grammar gym, the unit test, the world map passport, drawing canvases, tap-to-translate glossary, autosave.
  • Teacher dashboard: tobiasrugger.github.io/readings/this-is-how/dashboard.html
  • Optional paper: press Print in the app and choose blank packet for a handwriting version with ruled lines.
  • Colored pencils for the paper map, if you still want the physical coloring step alongside the digital passport.

Before you start

  1. Open the dashboard, go to Answer key, and fill in the short answers that depend on the books (names, bedroom counts, chores). Thirty-two rows. It takes about ten minutes with both books open, and after that every short answer in the class scores itself.
  2. Decide whether students choose their own level or you assign it. The switch is in the top bar and it saves with their work.
  3. Tell students to use the same email every time. That is the key their work saves under.

Pacing

Eight lessons, roughly one class period each, plus two days for the final project and one for the test. Stretch to three weeks if you run it as stations.

Day 1 Who is in these books?

Language: superlatives (oldest, youngest, closest) · Reading: inferring from a cover

  • Hold up both covers. Students write what they see before they know anything about the books.
  • Teach infer against guess: an inference uses evidence you can point to. Ask students to point at the clue.
  • Match the seven children of This Is How We Do It to their countries. This is the only self-checking match in the unit — students can retry.
  • World map passport. Eighteen countries across both books. Students tap a country and record the child's name and one detail. The dot turns gold when the row is complete. This runs across the whole unit, not just today — send them back to it after every lesson.

Watch for: students writing more young instead of younger. Worth a quick chart on the board.

Day 2 This is where I live

Language: there is / there are, prepositions in / on / at · Reading: reading captions for numbers

  • Counting questions first (how many bedrooms, how many levels) because they are answerable by everyone.
  • Then the naming questions, which need caption reading.
  • Production: This is where I lived / This is where I live. The past-then-present pair is the emotional heart of the whole unit. Do not rush it.

Watch for: students who left a home they will not describe. Let them write about a grandparent's house, a neighborhood, or the place they remember best. There is no requirement to be literal.

Day 3 This is who I live with

Language: possessive 's, possessive adjectives, -er comparisons

  • The families in these books include a child with two fathers, children with single mothers, children with grandparents. Name each one plainly as a family. That is the lesson.
  • Seven find-it questions, then a comparison to the student's own family.
  • Drawing: their family, with names labeled. The labels are the possessive practice.

Watch for: Neyser family for Neyser's family. Very common for Chinese and Vietnamese speakers.

Day 4 This is how I play

Language: third-person -s (he plays / they play)

  • Six questions across both books: Italy, Japan, horseback riding, soccer.
  • Two personal questions (Have you ever ridden a horse?, Do children play freeze tag in your country?) that reliably produce talk. Let them run long.
  • Production: how I played / how I play.

Extension: have students teach the class one game from home. Then write the rules as a numbered list — imperatives, a whole second grammar lesson for free.

Day 5 This is how I help

Language: simple past, regular -ed and irregular

  • Three questions from This Is How I Do It, then three cross-text questions from This Is How We Do It (Kei, Kian, Ribaldo). First time students hold both books at once.
  • Chore vocabulary is the densest in the unit: tend the cornfields, hang clothes to dry, do the laundry. All glossed and tappable.

Watch for: many of our students do far more at home than the children in the books — translating, childcare, work. When they write it, take it seriously in the discussion.

Day 6 This is how I get to school

Language: by bus / on foot, How does he get to school?

  • Shortest lesson. Good day to catch up on the map passport and the grammar gym.
  • Production: how they got to school before and how they get to Galileo now. Times and distances make a nice quick data set — graph the class commute on the board.

Watch for: by foot. Everybody says it. It is always on foot.

Day 7 Two books, one world

Language: both, also, but, while, -er than · Reading: comparing two texts

  • Peru is in both books. That is the hinge of the lesson — two children, one country, different lives. Ask what that proves.
  • Three same / three different, then the comparison paragraph. This is the first real paragraph of the unit and the rubric is on the page.
  • Challenge students answer why did the author write a second book? with evidence.

Days 8–9 This is how I do it — my page

Language: everything, in production

  • Eight panels, each a sentence frame plus a drawing: where I live, who I live with, how I get to school, what I eat, how I play, how I help, what I learn, how I go to sleep.
  • Then a closing paragraph: what do you want readers to know about your life?
  • Print with this page only to produce the physical page. Bind the class set into a book. Put it in the library.
The thing that makes this land: the class book goes on a shelf where other students can find it. Tell them that on day one, not day eight.

Day 10 Unit test

Twenty auto-scored questions (comprehension, vocabulary, grammar) plus one paragraph scored with the rubric. Books closed for the twenty; books open for the paragraph if you like. Scores appear in the dashboard as soon as students press Score my test.

The level switch changes the task, not the topic. Everyone answers the same question about the same page of the same book.

Scaffolds built into the app

For comprehension

  • Tap any highlighted word for a plain-English definition and a translation into Spanish, Chinese, Arabic, Vietnamese, Thai, or Nepali.
  • Vocabulary chips at the top of every lesson, pre-taught before the reading.
  • Every question names the page and the spread to look at.
  • Worked example answers carried over from the paper packets (The person who lives in Nepal is Susadh.).

For production

  • Sentence frames under every open question.
  • Help me start opens a list of sentence starters.
  • Grammar chart sits directly above the grammar practice, with immediate right/wrong feedback.
  • Drawing canvas on eleven questions, so a student with no English yet still produces an answer.
  • Ruled writing lines in print mode for students who write better by hand.

Running it as stations

If you would rather run this in the station model, one lesson splits cleanly into five:

  • Vocabulary — the word chips and the glossary, plus the matching grammar gym section.
  • Reading and responding — the find-it questions for that spread.
  • Listening and speaking — students interview each other with that lesson's question (Who did you live with?) and report back in third person, which forces the -s.
  • Proofreading — print two students' answers with the blank-packet option, swap, and mark capitals, periods, and subject–verb agreement.
  • Media response — the drawing canvas, or a photo from home with a caption in the sentence frame.

If you only have one book

Lessons 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 work from This Is How I Do It alone. Lesson 5 needs both, but the three cross-text questions can be answered from photocopies of the "how I help" spread of This Is How We Do It. Lesson 7 needs both. If you truly only have one, replace Lesson 7 with a comparison between two children inside the same book — the grammar is identical.
