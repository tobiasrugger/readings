ACTIVITY 3 OF 10

Subjects and verbs

This is the most useful skill in the whole book. If you can find the subject and the verb, you can fix fragments, fix agreement, and build compound sentences. Everything after this depends on it.

A sentence needs two things

The subject tells who or what does something. The verb tells the action (run, study, build) or the condition (is, was, seems).

A simple sentence has one subject–verb pair. But the subject can be two people, and the verb can be two actions:

My sister sings.  →  S V
My sister and my cousin sing.  →  S S V
My sister writes and sings.  →  S V V
My sister and my cousin write and sing.  →  S S V V

All four are simple sentences, because there is only one subject–verb pair.

ACTIVITY 4 OF 10

Subject–verb agreement

You already know my sister is and my sisters are. The trouble starts when words get between the subject and the verb. Here is the trick that solves almost every case.

Cross out the prepositional phrase

A prepositional phrase can sit between the subject and the verb. It is never the subject. Cross it out in your head:

One (of my sisters) is a nurse. → the subject is one, not sisters.
The color (of her eyes) changes in the sun. → the subject is color, not eyes.

Four groups to memorize

Always singular: one, each, either, neither, much, nothing, anyone, everyone, somebody

Always plural: both, several, many, few

It depends — look inside the phrase: some, all, most, none, a lot
Some (of the rice) was gone.  ·  Some (of the students) were gone.

Sentences starting with there: the subject comes after the verb. Look ahead.
There is one chair.  ·  There are four chairs.

ACTIVITY 5 OF 10

Fragments

In some languages you can leave out the subject, and in others you can leave out the verb. In English you can never leave out either one. A sentence missing one of them is a fragment, and it counts as an error.

Two kinds of fragment

No subject: Is very expensive in this neighborhood. → Rent is very expensive in this neighborhood.

No verb: The students in the back row always noisy. → The students in the back row are always noisy.

One exception: a command has no written subject. Sit down. Listen carefully. The subject you is understood.

ACTIVITY 6 OF 10

Time order

A narrative paragraph tells a story. The sentences go in the order the events happened — that is called time order. Special words called time order signals tell the reader where they are in the story.

Signals come in two shapes

Single words: first, next, later, then, soon, now, meanwhile, finally

Phrases: at last, after a while, after that, the next day, in the morning, at 6:00, before the game, two years ago

Put a comma after a signal that comes before the subject: Finally, the bus arrived.
Exception — then, soon, and now usually get no comma: Then the bus arrived.

ACTIVITY 8 OF 10

Compound sentences

A simple sentence has one subject–verb pair. A compound sentence glues two simple sentences together with a comma and a joining word. This is how you stop writing like a beginner.

The formula

S V  ,  and / but / so / or   S V

The bus was late, so I walked to school.

and — the two parts are alike  ·  but — the two parts are opposite
so — the second part is the result  ·  or — the two parts are choices

There are seven of these joining words in English: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. Their first letters spell FANBOYS. You will study the last three next semester.

Part A — Choose the joining word

Read the meaning of the two halves before you choose.

Part B — Simple or compound?

The comma test

A comma goes before the joining word only when there is a full subject and verb on both sides.

We ate lunch, and then we went home. → compound, comma
We ate lunch and then went home. → simple sentence (S V V), no comma

Mark each sentence, and the comma will be added for you if it needs one.

ACTIVITY 9 OF 10

Three comma rules

You have met two of these already. Here is the complete set for this unit. Every comma you need in your narrative paragraph comes from one of these three rules.

Rule 1 — after a time signal that starts the sentence

Yesterday, I studied for four hours.
At 8:00, I fell asleep on the couch.
Soon, I started snoring. → Soon I started snoring. (then / soon / now take no comma)

Rule 2 — before the joining word in a compound sentence

I was exhausted, so I took a break.
I woke up, and finished my homework. → I woke up and finished my homework. (simple sentence)

Rule 3 — between items in a list of three or more

I grabbed my keys, my phone, and my backpack.
Red, and gold are our school colors. → Red and gold are our school colors. (only two items)
