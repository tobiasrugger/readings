The three parts of a paragraph

In Chapters 1 and 2 you learned what a paragraph looks like and how to tell a story in one. Now you learn how a paragraph is built on the inside. Ten activities, in order, ending with a paragraph you plan and write yourself.

Think of a paragraph as a sandwich

TOPIC SENTENCE
SUPPORTING SENTENCES
CONCLUDING SENTENCE

The topic sentence and the concluding sentence are the two pieces of bread. The supporting sentences are the filling — and the filling is the part people actually come for.

ACTIVITY 1 OF 10

Find the three parts

Every academic paragraph has a topic sentence, several supporting sentences, and a concluding sentence. Read the paragraph below, then label each sentence.

What each part does

Topic sentence — tells the reader what the paragraph will discuss. Usually first.

Supporting sentences — give the details, examples, and reasons. This is most of the paragraph.

Concluding sentence — closes the paragraph by summarizing or restating the main idea in different words. Usually last.

ACTIVITY 2 OF 10

Topic and controlling idea

A topic sentence has two jobs. The topic names the subject. The controlling idea tells the reader what the paragraph will say about that subject — and it limits the paragraph to that one point.

Same topic, different controlling ideas

Street food in Bangkok is cheaper than cooking at home.
Street food in Bangkok is safer than most tourists expect.
Street food in Bangkok disappears after midnight.

Same topic three times. Three completely different paragraphs, because the controlling idea decides what goes in the middle.

ACTIVITY 4 OF 10

Too general, too specific, or just right

A topic sentence sits in the middle. Too wide and the reader learns nothing. Too narrow and you have already used up your only detail.

The three cases

Too general — Phones are useful. No controlling idea. The paragraph could go anywhere.

Too specific — My phone battery lasted nine hours yesterday. That is a detail. It belongs in the middle of the paragraph, not at the top.

Just right — A phone can replace three things a student used to carry. It tells the reader what is coming without giving away the details.

ACTIVITY 6 OF 10

The sentence that does not belong

A paragraph has unity when every sentence supports the topic sentence. One sentence in each paragraph below wanders off. Tap it.

How to find it

Read the topic sentence again, then ask of each sentence: does this help prove that? The intruder is often true and often interesting. That is what makes it hard. Being true is not enough — it has to be relevant.

ACTIVITY 7 OF 10

Closing the paragraph

A concluding sentence signals the end and reminds the reader of the main idea. It does not add new information, and it does not copy the topic sentence word for word.

Conclusion signals

These take a comma: All in all, · In brief, · In conclusion, · In short, · In summary, · Indeed, · To conclude, · To summarize, · To sum up,

These take no comma, because the sentence continues through them: It is clear that… · These examples show that… · You can see that…

ACTIVITY 8 OF 10

Apostrophes, part one: contractions

An apostrophe has three jobs in English. The first is to show where letters have been removed.

The apostrophe marks the missing letters

is not → isn't  ·  they are → they're  ·  she is → she's  ·  will not → won't

Notice won't. It does not follow the pattern, and you simply have to remember it.

We use contractions when we speak and when we write to friends. We usually do not use them in academic writing. Ask your teacher what he wants.

The trap that catches everyone

it's = it is. its = belonging to it.

It's raining.  →  It is raining. ✓
The dog lost its collar.  →  The dog lost it is collar. ✗

The test: say it is out loud. If the sentence still works, use it's. If it sounds wrong, use its with no apostrophe.

Possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe: my, your, his, her, its, our, their.

ACTIVITY 9 OF 10

Apostrophes, part two: possessives

The second job of the apostrophe is to show ownership. English gives you two ways to say it, and the rule for where the apostrophe goes depends on one question: does the word already end in s?

Two ways to show ownership

the orders of the doctor  =  the doctor's orders

Use 's more often for living owners. Use of the more often for objects: the leg of the table, not the table's leg.

When the owner is named, you have no choice — you must use the apostrophe: Maria's book, never the book of Maria.

Where does the apostrophe go?

Add 's — to singular nouns, and to plurals that don't end in s.
a child's toy · the children's game · the men's team · my boss's office

Add the apostrophe alone — to plural nouns that already end in s.
the students' club · the teachers' lounge · the Smiths' house

So the girl's bag is one girl. The girls' bag is several girls. The apostrophe is doing real work — it tells the reader how many people there are.

Outline it, then write it

An outline is an architect's plan. Nobody builds a house and works out where the kitchen goes afterward. Plan first, and the paragraph almost writes itself.
