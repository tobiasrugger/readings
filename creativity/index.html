Part one

Creativity is not a talent

Many people believe creativity is a gift. You either have it or you don’t. John Cleese disagrees. He says creativity is not a talent. It is a way of working — and anyone can learn it.Many people think creativity is a special gift. Some people have it. Some people don’t. John Cleese says no. Creativity is not a talent. It is a way of working. Anyone can learn it.

He likes to talk about research by a psychologist named Donald MacKinnon, who studied very creative architects and other professionals. The most creative people were not smarter than the others. The difference was how they spent time with a problem: they played with it longer before they decided.☀A psychologist studied very creative people. They were not smarter than other people. They did one thing differently: they played with a problem for a longer time.☀

Part two

Two ways to work

Cleese says we work in two modes. In closed mode, we are busy and focused on finishing. We feel some pressure, maybe some stress. Closed mode is good for getting things done — but it is bad for getting new ideas.Cleese says there are two ways to work. The first way is closed mode. You are busy. You want to finish fast. You feel some pressure. Closed mode is good for finishing work. It is bad for new ideas.

In open mode, we are relaxed, curious, and playful.☀ We are not in a hurry, and we don’t mind looking a little silly. This is where new ideas come from.The second way is open mode. You are relaxed. You are curious. You are playful.☀ You are not in a hurry. New ideas come from open mode.

In his book about creativity, Cleese uses a picture from another writer: a hare and a tortoise. The hare brain is fast. It runs straight to the finish line. The tortoise mind is slow. It wanders and notices things the hare runs past. We need both: open mode to find an idea, closed mode to finish it — and then back to open mode again.Cleese explains it with two animals. The hare is fast. It runs to the finish line. The tortoise is slow. It wanders and looks around. It sees things the hare does not see. We need both animals. First open mode to find an idea. Then closed mode to finish it.

Part three

The biggest killer: interruption

When someone asked Cleese what kills creativity most, his answer was simple: interruption.✦ Every time your phone buzzes, or someone asks you a quick question, or you check a message, your mind is pulled out of open mode. And it does not come back right away. It needs time to settle again.Someone asked Cleese: what kills creativity the most? His answer: interruption.✦ Your phone buzzes. Someone asks a question. You check a message. Your mind leaves open mode. It does not come back fast. It needs time to settle.

So Cleese says to protect two things. Space: find a place where nobody will disturb you. Time: decide when you will start and when you will stop.☀ Inside that space and that time, you are safe to play.So Cleese says: protect two things. Space: go to a place where nobody will disturb you. Time: choose a start time and a stop time.☀ Now you are safe to play with ideas.

At first, your mind will try to escape. You will suddenly remember a message you need to send, or a snack you want. Cleese says this is normal. If you stay, your mind slowly gets quiet — and then the ideas start to come.At first, your mind wants to escape. You remember a message. You want a snack. This is normal. Stay! Slowly your mind gets quiet. Then the ideas come.

Part four

Don’t take the first idea

Here is the strange part. Not knowing the answer feels uncomfortable. Most of us want to escape that feeling, so we grab the first answer we think of. The most creative people can stay uncomfortable a little longer.☀ They keep playing with the problem, and they often find something better.Not knowing the answer feels uncomfortable. We don’t like that feeling. So we take the first answer fast. Creative people wait a little longer.☀ They keep playing. They often find a better answer.

This does not mean you never decide. A deadline is real, and you must finish on time. But Cleese says: don’t decide before you need to.You still need to decide. A deadline is real. But don’t decide too early.

Part five

Mistakes are allowed. Laughing helps.

Cleese says nothing stops creativity like the fear of making a mistake.✦ When you are playing with ideas, nothing is wrong. A silly idea can lead to a good one. If you are afraid of looking stupid, you will stay in closed mode.Cleese says the biggest stop sign is fear of a mistake.✦ When you play with ideas, nothing is wrong. A silly idea can lead to a good idea.

Humor helps too. Laughing moves us from closed mode to open mode faster than anything.☀ Cleese points out that serious is not the same as solemn. You can talk about a very serious problem and still laugh. Laughing doesn’t make the work less important. It makes our minds more open.Humor helps too. Laughing moves you to open mode fast.☀ You can work on a serious problem and still laugh. Serious does not mean solemn. Laughing opens your mind.

Part six

Working with other people

Other people can help us create, or they can stop us. Cleese warns that if someone makes you feel defensive, you lose open mode.✦ When you feel judged, your mind closes like a door. You stop playing and start protecting yourself.Other people can help you create. Or they can stop you. If someone makes you feel defensive, you lose open mode.✦ You feel judged. Your mind closes like a door. You stop playing.

So in a good group, nobody attacks ideas while the group is still playing. One way to do this comes from improv comedy: answer every idea with “Yes, and…” instead of “No, but…”☀ During a brainstorm, every idea gets written down. Later, in closed mode, the group chooses the best ideas and makes them better.In a good group, nobody attacks ideas. Try this rule from improv comedy: say “Yes, and…” — not “No, but…”☀ In a brainstorm, write down every idea. Later, choose the best ideas.

And when someone gives you feedback, you can keep the door open too. Instead of arguing, try saying: “Thank you. Can you tell me more?”When someone gives you feedback, keep the door open. Don’t argue. Say: “Thank you. Can you tell me more?”

Part seven

Sleep on it

Cleese tells a story about this. He once wrote a comedy script and then lost it. He was upset, so he sat down and wrote it again from memory. Later he found the first version — and the new one was better.☀ While he wasn’t thinking about it, his mind had kept working.Cleese wrote a comedy script. Then he lost it! He was upset. He wrote it again from memory. Later he found the first one. The new one was better.☀ His mind kept working while he rested.

That is why he likes to sleep on it: work on a problem, stop, and look at it again the next day. Our minds keep working, even when we rest.So he likes to sleep on it. Work on a problem. Stop. Look again tomorrow. Your mind works even when you rest.
