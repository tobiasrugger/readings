Farewell to Manzanar by Jeanne Wakatsuki Houston and James D. Houston.

In 1942 the United States government forced 120,000 Japanese Americans out of their homes and into ten prison camps. Two out of every three were American citizens. No one was charged with a crime. No one had a trial.

Jeanne Wakatsuki was seven years old. Her family spent three and a half years at a camp called Manzanar, in the California desert. Thirty years later she wrote this book about it.

Our big question for this unit: What does a person lose when a country decides they do not belong — and what can they keep?

What happened after the camps

The camps closed in 1945. Most families came home to nothing — their houses, farms and boats were gone. For almost forty years, almost nobody talked about it.

In 1988 the United States government formally apologized and paid $20,000 to each surviving person who had been imprisoned. President Reagan signed the law. Many of the people it was meant for had already died.

Jeanne Wakatsuki Houston has said she could not write this book for many years because she felt ashamed — and that the shame itself was part of what the camps did to people.
