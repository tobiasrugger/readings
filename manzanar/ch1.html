Before You Read

Where and when: Long Beach, California. December 1941. Jeanne Wakatsuki is seven years old. Her father is a fisherman. Her family is Japanese American.

What happened on December 7, 1941: Japanese planes attacked a U.S. Navy base in Hawaii called Pearl Harbor. The United States entered World War II the next day. Suddenly, 120,000 Japanese Americans living on the West Coast were treated as enemies — even though most of them were born in America.

A word about the narrator: This is a memoir. A grown woman is remembering what she saw and did not understand when she was seven. Watch for the two voices: the child who is excited, and the adult who knows what is coming.

Part 1 — The boats go out

It was December 1941. I was seven years old. My family lived in Long Beach, California. It was a Sunday, so I was not at school. The sky was clear and blue.

My father — we called him Papa — had a fishing boat. Papa was shouting orders at the other men. He liked to give orders. My big brothers, Bill and Woody, worked on the boat. They checked the nets. They checked the gas.

Papa's boat was long and white. It cost $25,000. Papa wore rubber boots and a dark red sweater. My mother knitted the sweater for him. People called him “Skipper.” Papa liked that.

Papa did not own the boat alone. A fish company gave him the money for the boat. Every month he had to give some money back. Papa hated this. He wanted to be his own boss. All the fishermen wanted to be their own boss. But they also helped each other. They shared nets. They shared radios. They competed, but they also cooperated.

On that first weekend in December, there were 25 fishing boats leaving the harbor. I was seven years old. I remember that it was a Sunday because I was not in school. It was a clear and beautiful day. It was 1941 and we lived in Long Beach, California.

Papa was yelling orders at all of the other men who were going out to fish that Sunday. Papa loved to give orders because he went to a military school. My oldest brothers are named Bill and Woody. They worked for him on his boat. As part of Papa's crew, they would check the fishing nets and the gas tanks. They would also run to the grocery store for cigarettes.

Papa's boat was called The Nereid. It was long and white. It was worth $25,000 before the war. When he stood steering the boat, he looked like he was the commander of all the other fishing boats. Papa had a mustache then. He wore rubber boots and a dark red sweater that my mother knitted for him. He liked it when people called him “Skipper.”

In order to pay for The Nereid, he made a deal with one of the companies that buys and sells his fish. He had to give part of the money he made to the company because they gave him the money to buy the boat. He didn't like this because he didn't want to work for anyone. Many of the other fishermen around Long Beach had the same contract with the companies. Just like most Japanese men, all the fishermen wanted to be their own boss. But they all liked to fish together. They helped each other find the fish, and shared nets and radio equipment. They competed with each other to catch the most fish, but they also cooperated.

Part 2 — The boats come back

That Sunday, 25 boats went out to the ocean. We stood on the shore. Mama was there. Chizu was there. We waved goodbye. We shouted, “Good luck!” The boats got smaller and smaller.

Far away, the boats looked like little white birds on the horizon. The sun was bright. We had to squint our eyes.

Then something strange happened. The boats stopped. Then they got bigger. The boats were coming back.

“They're coming back,” Mama said. “Why?” asked Chizu. “Maybe the engine broke.” “Maybe someone is hurt.” “But they would not all come back,” said Mama. There was no explanation. This never happened before.

A man from the fish factory ran to us. He shouted, “The Japanese bombed Pearl Harbor!” Chizu asked Mama, “What is Pearl Harbor?” Mama asked the man, “What is Pearl Harbor?” But the man ran away. He did not have time to explain.

We never knew how many days the men would be out fishing. Sometimes they were gone for a week. Other times they would be gone for a month. On that Sunday, we waved goodbye to the men. My mother was there. Bill's wife was there. Woody's wife, Chizu, was there also. We yelled at them to have a good trip. Later, the boats were too far out in the ocean to hear us. But we continued to wave. We thought maybe it would give them good luck if we watched them until we couldn't see them anymore.

The boats would look like a row of seagulls flying on the horizon. We would have to squint our eyes because of the sun. Every time we blinked our eyes, we could expect them to be gone.

But on this Sunday in December, 1941, the boats never disappeared. They just stopped at the horizon. Then the little dots began to grow. The seagulls turned back into boats. The line of white boats was coming back to us.

“They're coming back,” my mother said. “Why would they be coming back?” Chizu said. “Maybe the engine,” one of the women yelled. “Maybe somebody got hurt,” another woman said softly. “But they wouldn't all come back,” Mama said. She was very confused.

Another woman said, “Maybe there is a storm coming.” They all looked at the sky. It was a beautiful day and it was not going to rain. Mama shook her head. There was no explanation for this. It had never happened before. We watched and waited. When the boats were about half a mile away, a man from the fish factory ran to us. He was shouting that the Japanese had just bombed Pearl Harbor.

Chizu said to Mama, “What does he mean? What is Pearl Harbor?” Mama asked the man, “What is Pearl Harbor?” But the man ran away, bringing the news to everyone. He didn't have time to explain.

Part 3 — Papa burns the flag

That night, Papa burned his flag from Japan. He had carried that flag for thirty-five years. It was beautiful. I was surprised.

He also burned letters and documents. He burned everything that showed a connection to Japan.

But it did not help. Papa was not a U.S. citizen. He had a license for his boat, so the government already had his name. The FBI was taking away Japanese fishermen. They thought the fishermen were talking to enemy ships. Papa knew the FBI would come.

When he got home that night, Papa burned the flag that he had brought from Japan when he moved to the U.S. thirty-five years earlier. It was a beautiful flag. I was surprised that he burned it. He also burned a lot of papers and other documents. He burned anything that would show that he had a connection to Japan.

But all of this didn't help. He was not a citizen of the U.S., but he had a license to own and run a fishing boat. The government gave him the license, so they knew a lot about him. The FBI was picking up and questioning all Japanese men like my father who owned fishing boats. They thought that these fishermen could be talking to the enemy ships out in the ocean. Papa knew that the FBI would come to question him.

Part 4 — The FBI searches the houses

Two weeks later, the FBI came. We were staying at a friend's house. Many Japanese families lived there.

The FBI searched the houses. They made a big mess. They looked for radios. They looked for anything from Japan. Many houses had radios, because the women talked to the men on the fishing boats. The FBI were suspicious of every radio.

The FBI men were new. They did not know what they were doing. They thought a flashlight was dangerous. They thought a kitchen knife was dangerous. They thought a camera and a toy sword were dangerous!

Two weeks later, they came for my father. We were staying at our friend's house where most of the Japanese families lived. The FBI were questioning all the people there. Sometimes they looked through people's houses and made a huge mess. They were looking for anything that could be used to send a signal to a plane or a large boat. They were also looking for anything that showed that the family was loyal to the Emperor of Japan.

Most of the houses had radios and large antenna towers on their roofs. This was because the women wanted to be able to talk to the men on their fishing boats when they were out at sea. The FBI were suspicious of all houses that had a radio and an antenna.

The men who worked for the FBI were not very professional. The FBI just hired a lot of men after the Japanese bombed Pearl Harbor to search these homes. They didn't know what they were doing, and they thought that simple household items like flashlights, kitchen knives, cameras and toy swords could be used to help the enemy!

Part 5 — They take Papa

Papa knew they would arrest him. He did not hide. He did not resist.

Two FBI men came. They wore big hats and long coats, like in a movie. They arrested Papa. He did not fight. There was no reason to fight.

Papa was a man without a country. He was born in Japan. Japan was at war with America. He lived in America for thirty-five years, but the law did not let him become a citizen. Now he had no rights. And he looked like the enemy.

Papa lost everything — but not his dignity. He was tall, almost six feet. He was over 50. He had ten children. When the FBI pushed him toward the door, he stopped them. He made them walk behind him.

Papa knew that he was going to be arrested by the FBI. He didn't try to hide or resist. Two FBI men with big hats and long coats, like in the movies, arrested Papa. He didn't struggle. There was no reason to struggle. He was a man without a country. He was born in Japan, and Japan was at war with America. However, after thirty-five years of living in the U.S., he was not allowed to become an American citizen because of the laws. He was now a man with no rights, and he looked exactly like the enemy.

Papa lost everything except his dignity. He was very tall for a Japanese man — almost six feet tall, lean and muscular. He had healthy skin from being at sea. He was over 50 years old. He had ten children. He had had a lot of hard times living in the U.S. When he moved here, he was sure that his life would be good. But life in the U.S. was hard, and he was no longer so sure that he would have a lot of success. But he still had his dignity. When the FBI men tried to push him out the door, he made them stop. He made them walk behind him when he walked out the door.

Part 6 — Mama cries

Mama waited. The FBI took the men to a building for interrogation. Some men came home after a few hours. Papa did not come home that night. He did not come home the next day.

Then we learned the truth. Papa was arrested and sent far away. Where? For how long? Nobody told us. My brothers asked many questions. Nobody answered.

The next day, a newspaper said why. The paper said Papa gave oil to Japanese submarines near the California coast.

My mother started to cry. She cried for many days. Mama was a small, round woman. She laughed a lot. But we never saw her cry like this. I held her legs. I wanted to know why everyone was crying.

I did not cry for Papa. I did not understand. I saw my father again one year later. Then I understood why Mama cried.

Mama knew that the FBI were taking all of the men to an interrogation center close to where most of the Japanese families lived. Some of them were released after the FBI asked them questions. At first, Mama was not very worried. Maybe she did not allow herself to be worried. When it got dark that night, Papa was not back. The next day Papa was still not back. Finally, someone told us that he was arrested and sent away. Where did he go? How long would he be gone? We did not know. My brothers tried to find out what happened to Papa, but they got no answers.

What reason did they use for arresting Papa? What did they charge them with? We didn't know. The next day, there was an article in the Santa Monica newspaper that explained why Papa was arrested. The article said that Papa was arrested for delivering oil to Japanese submarines not far from the California coast.

My mother started to cry. She cried for many days. She was a small, round woman who usually laughed and sometimes cried. But we had never seen her cry like this before. I didn't know why she was crying so much. I held on to her legs. I wanted to know why everyone was crying.

This was the beginning of a terrible and confusing time for everyone in my family. I didn't cry about Papa. I didn't understand why my Mama was so sad. The next time I saw my father was a year later. Then I understood why my mother cried that day.

Grammar: The Simple Past

This whole chapter happened in 1941. It is finished. So almost every verb is in the simple past.

KindHow to make itFrom Chapter 1
Regularverb + -edwave → waved   shout → shouted   arrest → arrested
Irregularthe word changesgo → went   come → came   see → saw   hold → held
Negativedid not + base verbHe did not fight. (NOT did not fought)
