Before You Read

Where we left off: The FBI took Papa away. Jeanne is seven. Mama has ten children and no husband at home.

Three places in this chapter: Terminal Island → downtown Los Angeles → Manzanar. The family moves three times in three months.

Executive Order 9066: On February 19, 1942, President Roosevelt signed this order. It let the Army force any person out of the West Coast without a trial. About 120,000 Japanese Americans were moved to ten camps. Two out of every three were American citizens.

Shikata ga nai (しかたがない) is Japanese for “it cannot be helped.” It is the title Jeanne gives this chapter in the book. Keep it in mind while you read.

Part 1 — Moving to Terminal Island

Papa was gone. I was seven. I was not very scared about Papa. I was scared about all the changes in my life.

Before the war, we lived in a big, beautiful house near Santa Monica. We were the only Japanese family on the street.

Then Mama moved us to Terminal Island. She wanted to be near other Japanese people. My brother Woody already lived there.

But I was scared of Japanese people. Why? Papa used to say, “If you don't behave, I will sell you to the Chinese.” I believed him. I carried that fear with me.

When Papa left home in 1941, I wasn't that scared about Papa. I was more scared about all of the changes in my life.

Papa was good at everything he did. When I was born he was a farmer. Later he became a fisherman and we moved to Ocean Park, near Santa Monica. We lived in the same big beautiful house until the FBI arrested Papa. We were the only Japanese family in the neighborhood. When Papa disappeared, Mama became very scared and wanted to live closer to other Japanese people, so she decided to move us to Terminal Island. Woody already lived there, and she wanted to keep the entire family together.

For the first time, I had to live with other Japanese people as my neighbors, and this was very scary for me. My fear was because of Papa. He used to tell me and my brothers and sisters that he was going to sell us to Chinese people if we didn't behave. Because he said these things, I was very scared of Asian people. I had this fear with me when we moved to Terminal Island.

Part 2 — School on Terminal Island

Terminal Island was a town for factory workers. The men caught fish. The women cleaned the fish and put them in cans. Everybody worked day and night. Mama got a job right away. She left in the middle of the night to work.

Our new house was small and plain. It was like a shack. Thin walls. Rough wooden floors.

At school, kids bullied me. I did not speak Japanese. I only spoke English. The other Japanese kids hated me for that. I was afraid to go to school. My brother Kiyo waited for me at the door. He found a safe way home. The kids never hit us. But they made scary noises and talked in a language I did not know.

Terminal Island was a town that existed for factory workers to live in. The men were all fishermen, and the women worked to clean the fish and put them into cans. The people who lived there didn't have much money and worked all day and all night. Mama had to get a job as soon as we moved there. My mother would leave with Chizu in the middle of the night to go to work.

Our house was very small and very plain. It was like a shack. It had thin walls and rough wooden floors. It was much different from our house in Ocean Park.

I got bullied and bothered a lot at school. I did not speak any Japanese. I had only spoken English all my life. The students in my second grade class hated me for this. I really disliked going to school, because I knew they would attack me. My brother Kiyo, three years older, would wait for me at the door and figure out a safe way for us to run home. None of these kids actually attacked us. Still, they made us scared by making noises and talking to us in a language I could not understand.

Part 3 — Executive Order 9066

I felt scared for a long time. But it was only two months.

In February, the Navy closed Terminal Island. Most of us were born in America. But the Navy said it was dangerous to have so many Japanese people near a naval base. On February 25, they told us to leave. We had no choice.

The government moved us to downtown Los Angeles. President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. This gave the government power to remove people from the West Coast. People were afraid. Would the government put us all in camps?

Before we left, a letter came from Papa. He was in a prison camp far away, with other men the government called “the enemy.”

At the time, I felt like I had been scared for years. In fact, it was only two months. Late in February, the Navy decided to close Terminal Island completely. Most of us were American born, but they thought it was dangerous to have so many Asians living close to the naval base. We knew they wouldn't let us live together for a long time. However, like Papa's arrest, there was nothing we could do to stop it.

In our family there were four kids, my mother, and my grandmother. My grandmother was sixty-five, spoke no English, and could not see. My mother did not know where we would move next. On February 25, they told us we had to leave, and we had no choice.

The government made us move to an immigrant neighborhood in downtown Los Angeles. Many people from Terminal Island moved there. President Roosevelt had made a law called 9066. This gave the government the power to remove people from certain areas in the western part of America, where we lived. Some people thought they would put us into big camps, or make us move away. They thought something bad like this would happen to all Japanese Americans.

Just before we left Terminal Island, Mama got a letter from a far away place. The letter was from Papa. He said he had been put in a prison, a camp for all foreign men who were considered “the enemy.”

Part 4 — The teacher in Los Angeles

In Los Angeles, Mama and Woody worked for a man who sold celery. Kiyo, May and I went to school.

I do not remember my teacher's face. I remember that she felt far away from me. She never helped me. She never talked to me.

My old teacher in Ocean Park was kind. She went sailing on Papa's boat. She cried when we left.

This new teacher was different. This was the first time I felt that a white person hated me. America was afraid of Japan. There was already racism against Asians in California. Now it got much worse.

Once we moved to downtown Los Angeles, Mama and Woody got jobs working for a man who sold celery. Kiyo, my sister May and I went to school. I remember those few weeks because of my teacher. I do not remember what she looked like, but I remember how far away she seemed to me. It was like she was not connected to me.

In Ocean Park, my teacher was a kind old woman who sometimes went sailing on Papa's boat with us, and cried when we left. Here, this teacher did not seem to care about me at all. She would never help me when I needed help in class — she would not even talk to me.

This was the first time I ever felt like a white person hated me. Looking back, I can see what was happening. At this time in California, people were very scared of the Japanese. America became frightened by the war and stopped trusting Japanese people. There had already been strong racism toward Asians in California, but now the treatment of Japanese people became much worse.

Part 5 — The bus

We did not know what Manzanar was. The government made us go.

But we were a little relieved to leave. People were attacking Japanese homes in California. Manzanar seemed safer. Some of us even thought it was an adventure.

A bus picked us up at a Buddhist church. Someone put a number tag on my coat and on my bag. Our family got a number. That number was our name until we left the camp.

I had never left Los Angeles County. I had never ridden on a bus. I was excited. I fell asleep. Almost half the people on the bus were my relatives. Mama and my brothers worked hard to make that happen. Many other families were separated for months.

We did not know what Manzanar was when we went to it. We only went to Manzanar because the government made us. However, we were a little relieved when we left. We had heard that homes of Japanese families were being attacked in California. We did not want this to happen to us. Moving to an area that seemed safer, away from all of the white people, did not look like a bad idea. Some of us even thought it was an adventure.

The government picked us up at a Buddhist church in Los Angeles. It was very early when we arrived. We had our bags and heavy coats. Someone put a numbered tag on my coat and on my bag. Our family was given a number, and everyone used the number as our name until we left the camp.

I had never left Los Angeles County, never gone more than ten miles from the coast. I had never even ridden on a bus. I was very excited. I finally fell asleep. The bus felt very safe to me. Almost half of the passengers were relatives. Mama and my older brothers had made that happen, making sure most of us were on the same bus driving to the same camp. I did not know at the time that this had been very hard to arrange. Many families were not as lucky. Many families were separated for many months.

Part 6 — Arriving at Manzanar

We rode all day. We arrived late in the afternoon. The first thing I saw was a yellow swirl in front of a red sun. I heard rain on the bus. It was not rain. It was dust and sand.

We drove past a barbed wire fence. We went through a gate. I saw tents and long black barracks. People sat outside, waiting for their families. Everyone on our bus was quiet. I put my head out the window and yelled, “Hey, this whole bus is full of Wakatsukis!” The strangers smiled. Everyone laughed — even though they were sad and nervous.

After dinner we saw our new home. One barrack had six small rooms. Each room had one light bulb and one stove. Twelve people in my family got two rooms.

They gave us blankets and empty mattress covers. My brothers filled them with straw. We hung blankets to make walls.

Eight of us slept in one room: Granny, Lillian, Ray, May, Kiyo, Mama, and me. I was the youngest, so I slept in Mama's bed. I slept there every night until Papa came back.

We rode in the bus all day. It was late in the afternoon when we got to Manzanar. The first thing I saw was a yellow swirl against a red setting sun. It sounded like the bus was being rained on, but it was not rain. It was a large cloud of dust and sand.

We drove past a big barbed wire fence, through a gate, and into an open space where trunks and sacks and packages had been dumped. I could see some tents and the first black barracks. People were sitting around waiting for friends and relatives. I was excited. The people outside looked into our bus, looking for their families. Everybody in our bus was very, very quiet. I put my head out of the bus window and yelled, happily, “Hey, this whole bus is full of Wakatsukis!” Outside, the strangers smiled. Everyone laughed, even though they were sad and nervous.

After dinner, we went to our new home — the barracks. Each building was divided into six spaces. Each was about the size of one room, with one light bulb and a stove for heat. We were given two of these spaces for the twelve people in our family. We were given blankets and bed covers, which my brothers stuffed with straw.

Our first job was to find a space for everyone to sleep in the very small room. We took blankets and hung them up to divide the space. It was very difficult for the young couples, Bill and Tomi and Woody and Chizu. They had very little space to themselves, and fought a lot about noise and other problems.

The rest of the family squeezed into one room: Granny, Lillian, age fourteen, Ray, thirteen, May, eleven, Kiyo, ten, Mama, and me. It was not too hard for me at the time. Since I was the youngest, I got to sleep in Mama's bed. I slept with her each night until Papa came back.

Grammar: we / us · they / them

In Chapter 2 the narrator says we and they constantly. That is the whole point of the chapter: we (the Japanese families) and they (the government). You must choose the right form.

Subject — does the actionObject — receives the action
meI slept in Mama's bed.Papa told me a story.
usWe rode the bus all day.The government moved us.
themThey closed Terminal Island.We could not stop them.
her / himShe cried.   He waited.I slept with her.   I saw him.

Quick test: before the verb → subject (we, they, I, he, she). After the verb or after a word like to, for, with, at → object (us, them, me, him, her).

Practice A — Choose the right word

• (We / Us) moved three times in three months.
• The government moved (we / us) to Manzanar.
• (They / Them) put a number tag on my coat.
• We could not stop (they / them).
• My brother Kiyo waited for (I / me) at the door.
• (He / Him) found a safe way home.
• Mama worked at night, so we did not see (she / her) much.
• (She / Her) wanted to keep the family together.
