What is gratitude?

Gratitude means noticing the good things in your life. Sometimes you say thank you out loud. Sometimes you only think it. It is not only for big things. A warm bed is a good thing. A friend who waits for you is a good thing. Food you like is a good thing.

Most people only think about gratitude one time a year, in November. Scientists say that is a mistake.

A scientist asked a question

Sonja Lyubomirsky is a scientist in California. She wanted to know one thing: can a person become happier on purpose?

She did an experiment. First she measured how happy people felt. Then she gave them a job. Every week, write five things you are thankful for. Some people wrote one time each week. Other people wrote three times each week. They did this for six weeks. Then she measured their happiness again.

What she found

The people who wrote one time each week became happier. That part was not a surprise.

But the people who wrote three times each week did not change at all. Nothing happened to them.

Lyubomirsky thinks she knows why. Three times a week was too much. The people got bored. Writing became a chore, like homework. When something becomes a chore, it stops working.

So here is the rule for this class: we will do this once a week, and we will change how we do it. Sometimes you will write. Sometimes you will draw. Sometimes you will tell a person. Keeping it fresh is the whole point.

Three reasons it works

1. You slow down. When you write the good thing down, you live it a second time. Scientists call this savoring.

2. It helps on hard days. Gratitude does not make a hard life easy. But people who practice it can cope better. After September 11, 2001, researchers found that gratitude was the second most common feeling Americans reported — right after sympathy.

3. You stop taking things for granted. Humans get used to good things very fast. A new phone feels amazing for one week. Then it feels normal. Scientists call this hedonic adaptation, and it is the quiet enemy of happiness. Gratitude slows it down.

Two other ideas

Mindful breathing. When you are angry or stressed, your heart beats fast. Slow breathing lowers your heart rate and tells your body that you are safe. This is what apps like Headspace teach. We will practice it below.

Flow. A scientist named Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi studied people who love what they do. He found that people are happiest when a task is hard — but not too hard. You forget the time. You forget yourself. He called this flow. Think about the last time this happened to you.

Sources: Sonja Lyubomirsky, The How of Happiness, Chapter 4 · the Greater Good Science Center at UC Berkeley · Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Flow.
