3Read: what do monsters represent?

Read it once. Then read it again and tap the words that are new for you.

1Monsters represent the unknown, our deepest fears, and the eventual death that we all face. Throughout history, there are countless examples of monsters. Some of our most well-known monsters come from an age in which the world was still shrouded in darkness, counting nameless fears in the dark.

2That is why a monster is never only a monster. It is a symbol. When we look closely at a monster, we can see what the people who told the story were afraid of — and sometimes what we are still afraid of today.

1Monsters are symbols. They show what people are afraid of. They represent three things: things we do not know, our biggest fears, and death. Every person dies one day.

2There are countless monsters in history. Many famous monsters come from long ago. Then, people did not understand the world. The world was dark, and people had fears with no names.

3So a monster is not only a monster. When we look at a monster, we can see what people were afraid of. Sometimes we are still afraid of the same things today.

4Sixteen fears that monsters symbolize

Read the sixteen fears. Tap any word you do not know.

  1. Fear of medicine and doctors, or of science and technology
  2. A scary person who gets mad too much and attacks others
  3. A person who looks beautiful, young, and good, but is really bad
  4. A handsome or beautiful person who you like, but who seduces you, takes your energy, and makes you feel bad
  5. A scary memory that you want to forget but cannot forget
  6. A house where bad things happened — for example, parents were abusive, or someone was murdered
  7. A family with problems and trauma that pass from generation to generation — from grandparents to parents to children
  8. A memory that you want to forget, but it keeps coming back to your mind
  9. The fear that things you love, and think are nice and innocent, are secretly bad
  10. The fear that the world is so dangerous that you cannot escape, even in your own bedroom
  11. A person who is very good at cooking and singing, who can charm you and cast a spell over you
  12. A person who does not think, and just acts automatically
  13. A person who is addicted to drugs, a phone, or a computer, and cannot control themselves anymore — the addiction controls them
  14. Fear of disease — for example, COVID, Ebola, or AIDS
  15. Fear of powerful women, or hatred of strong women
  16. Fear that a big group of people who are different from you will take control and change your world — fear of people of another race, country, or class. This is called xenophobia.

6Monsters from around the world

Read at least four stories. If there is a story from your country, read that one first. Answer the question under each story you read.

La Llorona the weeping woman

MEXICO AND CENTRAL AMERICA

Long ago, a beautiful young woman named María had two children. Their father left her for another woman. María was so angry and so sad that she lost her mind. She threw her children into the river, and they drowned. The next day she understood what she did. She searched the river, but her children were gone. Soon she died too. Now her spirit walks next to rivers and lakes at night in a long white dress. She wails, "¡Ay, mis hijos!" — "Oh, my children!" Parents warn their children: if you go near the water at night, La Llorona will take you.

Why does La Llorona cry?

Aswang the neighbor at night

THE PHILIPPINES

In the daytime, an aswang looks like a normal person. Maybe she is a quiet neighbor who sells vegetables, or a man who does not talk much. But at night the aswang can shapeshift — it changes into a big black dog, a pig, or a bird. It hunts in the dark and makes a sound, "tik-tik-tik." Strangely, the sound is loud when the aswang is far away and quiet when it is close. Families put salt and garlic by the doors and windows to keep it out. People say that if you look into an aswang's eyes, your picture is upside down.

What does an aswang look like in the daytime?

Aisha Qandisha the woman by the water

MOROCCO

In Morocco, people tell stories about Aisha Qandisha, a jinniya — a female jinn. She lives near rivers, springs, and the sea. At night she looks like a very beautiful woman, and she calls men by their names. Men who follow her fall under her charm, and some never come back to their families. But she has one secret: under her long dress, her feet are the feet of a goat or a camel. Grandmothers say: if a beautiful woman calls your name by the water at night, look at her feet first — and then run.

What is Aisha Qandisha's secret?

The Ghoul the stranger in the desert

ARABIA

The English word ghoul comes from the Arabic word ghūl. Long before Islam, Arab travelers told stories about the ghūl of the desert. It can shapeshift — it can look like a hyena, an old friend, or a stranger who needs help. It calls to travelers in a kind voice and leads them far from the road, into the empty sand, where it eats them. Later stories say the ghoul also lives in cemeteries and eats the dead. Travelers learned to stay on the road and not to follow a friendly voice in the dark.

How does the ghoul catch travelers?

Jiangshi the hopping corpse

CHINA

A jiangshi is a dead body that cannot rest. Many stories say it is a person who died far from home and did not have the right funeral. Its body is stiff, so it cannot walk. It hops, with its arms straight out in front. It wears the clothes of an old Qing dynasty official. A jiangshi cannot see, but it can feel your breath. If you meet one, hold your breath and do not move! A Taoist priest can stop a jiangshi with a yellow paper charm on its forehead.

How can you hide from a jiangshi?

Gumiho the nine-tailed fox

KOREA

In Korea, a fox that lives for a thousand years grows nine tails and becomes a gumiho. A gumiho can shapeshift into a beautiful young woman. She is sweet and smart, and men fall in love with her. But she is not human. In many stories, the gumiho wants to become a real woman, and to do that she must eat the livers or hearts of people. In some newer stories and TV dramas, the gumiho is lonely and kind, and she only wants to be human and to be loved.

What does the gumiho want in many stories?

Ma Da the water ghost

VIETNAM

In Vietnam, when a person drowns, people say their spirit can become a ma da — a water ghost. The ma da is trapped under the water. It is cold and lonely, and it wants to be free. There is only one way out: it must pull another person under the water to take its place. So the ma da waits in rivers and ponds. When a child swims alone, it grabs the child's foot. That is why grandparents say: never swim alone, and never swim at noon or at night.

How can a ma da become free?

Mae Nak the wife who waited

THAILAND

Nak lived with her husband, Mak, near Bangkok. She was going to have a baby. Then Mak had to go to war. While he was away, Nak and the baby both died. When Mak came home, Nak was waiting for him at the door with the baby, smiling. Mak was happy. Neighbors tried to tell him, but everyone who spoke was punished. One day, Nak dropped a lime from the kitchen. Her arm grew very long and reached down under the house to pick it up. Then Mak knew — his wife was a ghost. Her love was stronger than death.

How did Mak learn the truth?

Baba Yaga the witch in the forest

RUSSIA AND EASTERN EUROPE

Deep in the forest lives Baba Yaga, an old witch with iron teeth. Her house stands on giant chicken legs and turns around and around. The fence around it is made of bones. She flies through the sky in a big bowl. Children know that Baba Yaga eats people. But she is not only bad. In the story of Vasilisa, a girl goes to Baba Yaga's house to ask for fire. She is brave, she works hard, and she is polite — and Baba Yaga helps her. If you are rude or lazy, she will eat you. If you are brave and kind, she may help you.

Who does Baba Yaga help?

Churel and Kichkandi the woman with backward feet

INDIA, PAKISTAN, AND NEPAL

In India and Pakistan, people tell stories about the churel. She was a woman who died unhappily — maybe during childbirth, or because her husband's family treated her badly. Now she comes back for revenge. At night she looks like a beautiful woman, and she calls young men to follow her. But her feet point backward. In Nepal, people tell a similar story about the kichkandi, who waits by the road at night. In both stories, the sign is the same: look at the feet.

How can you know a churel?

Dhegdheer the woman with the long ear

SOMALIA

Somali grandmothers tell stories about Dhegdheer — the name means "long ear." She was a powerful woman who lived far away from other people. One of her ears was very long, and she could hear people talking from far, far away. She ate people who came near her house. Dhegdheer had a daughter who was not like her. The daughter was afraid of her mother and ran away to a town. Children are told to be quiet at night — because Dhegdheer can hear everything.

What is special about Dhegdheer?

Loup-garou the wolf inside

FRANCE, QUEBEC, LOUISIANA, AND HAITI

The loup-garou is a person who turns into a wolf — a werewolf. In old French and Quebec stories, a person who does not go to church for seven years becomes a loup-garou and runs through the forest at night. In Louisiana it is called the rougarou. In Haiti, a lougawou is a person who takes off their skin at night, changes into an animal or a ball of fire, and goes out to hurt people. In the morning, the loup-garou is a normal person again, and nobody knows.

In the morning, what happens to the loup-garou?

C. Monster warnings: If you …, the monster will …

Write the verb in the correct form. Monsters give warnings: if you do this, the monster will do that.

If + present, … will + verb.   If you go out at night, La Llorona will take you.

1. If you (go) to the river at night, La Llorona (take) you.
2. If you (swim) alone, the Ma Da (pull) you under the water.
3. If a Jiangshi (hear) you breathe, it (find) you.
4. If you (be) rude to Baba Yaga, she (eat) you.
5. If a child (disobey) his parents, the monster (come) for him.
6. If you (leave) salt by the door, the Aswang (not / come in).

Teacher notes — Mr. Toby

Auto-scored (65): Part 2 words (10), Part 3 true/false (5), Part 6 story questions (12, one per story — students who read four stories will have four), Part 8 grammar (26), Part 10 multiple choice (12). Everything else is for reading on the dashboard.

Suggested pacing (5 days): Day 1 — survey, class results, words. Day 2 — the reading and the sixteen fears; model one monster (the ghost) out loud, then Part 5 in pairs, then the reveal. Day 3 — jigsaw Part 6: groups of four, each student reads three stories and teaches them; students from a story's country go first. Day 4 — Part 7 and grammar A–C. Day 5 — writing and Part 10.

Sensitive ground: fears 6 and 7 name abuse, murder, and family trauma. Students choose which fears to write about; nobody has to connect a fear to their own life. Fear 16 (xenophobia) is worth ten minutes of talk: the zombie horde is often how people describe immigrants. Let students name that themselves.

Belief: for some students, jinn and spirits are part of their religion, not folklore. Aisha Qandisha is told as a story about one jinniya, not about jinn in general. Say out loud that some people in the room believe, some do not, and both are respected.
