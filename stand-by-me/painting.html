Reading the Painting

This is a real painting. It hangs in our classroom. The artist read the same story you are reading, and then made this. Nothing in it is an accident — every color, every drip, every piece of paper was a choice. Your job today is to find those choices and say what they mean.

Name: _______________________ Period: ______ Date: __________

0 Look first. Don't write yet.

Look at the painting for two full minutes. No talking. No phone. Just your eyes.

Now write the first three words in your head. Any language.

1 Feeling

A painting does something to your body before you understand it. Start there.

How does this painting make you feel? Tap words to add them, or write your own.

Tap a word → it goes into the box below.

What exactly makes you feel that way? Point to one thing in the painting.

Two feelings at the same time? Many people feel two opposite things here. Which two?

2 Color

Cut the painting in half with your eyes. Top half. Bottom half.

What colors are in the top half? What colors are in the bottom half?

The top is a dark red-brown. Name three real things that are this exact color.

Need a hint?

Think about: dried blood. A bruise two days old. Red wine. Rust. Old meat. A crushed berry. Which one feels right for this story?

Now the pale green at the bottom. What is that color in real life?

Need a hint?

Grass at six in the morning. Fog. Mint. Hospital walls. The face of someone who is going to be sick. More than one answer can be true.

Usually a sky is light and the ground is dark. Here it is the opposite: the sky is heavy and dark, the ground is pale and light. What does that switch do to the painting?

Where is the gold? What is the gold on? In art, gold usually means something is precious or holy. Does that fit here?

3 The drips

The dark red does not stay in the sky. It drips down, over the grass, over the deer's legs, and over the side edge of the canvas. The artist saw this happen and let it happen.

Where do the drips start? Where do they stop?

What else in the world runs down like this? Check every one that fits.

The artist could have wiped the drips away before the paint dried. She kept them. Why?

Need a hint?

Ask yourself: does a drip look planned or accidental? Slow or fast? Controlled or out of control? Now think about the four boys walking to see a dead body. Is that experience planned and controlled?

Big question

There is no train in this painting. There is no body.

A boy is killed by a train in this story. But the artist painted a deer standing in the grass. So how does she put death into the painting without showing it?

4 Blueberries

Small round dots are everywhere: in the dark sky, on the deer's back, in the grass. Some are pale. Some are blue-grey. Find them before you answer.

How many dots can you find? What color are they? Where are most of them?

On the glued paper there is one word you can read clearly: blueberries. Think about a real blueberry. Answer all four:

Need a hint?

Squeeze a blueberry and it is gone in one second. The stain it leaves is dark red-purple. Now look at the top half of the painting again. Same color?

A blueberry is small, soft, dark, sweet, easy to crush, and good for only a short time. How is a blueberry like a child or a teenager?

Need a hint?

Useful words: delicate, fragile, vulnerable, innocence. A blueberry cannot protect itself. Neither can a twelve-year-old walking on a railroad track.

Text hunt

Find the blueberries in the story.

Blueberries are in the book. Go find them. Why was Ray Brower out near the tracks? What was he carrying? Copy the exact sentence and write the page number.

Can't find it?

He did not walk out there for no reason. He went out to pick something, and he brought something to put it in. Look at the part where the boys first hear about the missing boy — and look at the MISSING poster on our classroom wall.

Now go back to the dots. Knowing what you know now — what are those dots? Give two possible answers.

Need a hint?

A boy was carrying a pail of berries. A train hit him. What happened to the berries? Where did they go? Are they still in the air, or are they falling, or have they already landed?

5 The deer

Look at the head for thirty seconds before you write. Notice which way the body points and which way the face points. They are not the same direction.

Is the deer walking, standing, running, or turning? Where are her eyes looking?

Is she looking at you? How does that feel — and would it feel different if she were looking away?

Text hunt

Find the deer in the story.

Early one morning Gordie is awake alone, on watch, while the other boys sleep. A deer comes out. Find that part of the story. Copy one sentence, and write the page number.

After he sees the deer, does Gordie tell the other boys? Why or why not? What does that tell you about him?

Hardest question on this page: the artist painted a deer, not four boys, not a train, not a body. What do the deer and Ray Brower have in common?

Need a hint?
  • Both are alone, near the same railroad tracks.
  • Both are found by boys who were not supposed to be there.
  • Both are silent. Neither one can speak for itself.
  • Both are something wild and alive that a train can kill in one second.

The deer is the most beautiful thing Gordie sees on this trip. The body is the worst thing. They happen on the same weekend, two miles apart.

6 The words glued on her body

Write every word you can read. Broken words are fine — copy the broken part too.

The paper is glued on the deer's body — not on the sky, not on the grass. Why put the story on the animal itself?

Need a hint?

The story is called "The Body." What does it mean to write words on a living thing? Is the deer wearing the story, or is the story a wound on her, or is she made of the story?

Some words are painted over and you cannot read them. Why would an artist hide part of the text on purpose?

Need a hint?

Gordie is a grown man telling a story from when he was twelve. How much of a memory from long ago do you still have? All of it, or pieces? Do the missing pieces look like this?

7 How it was made

This is a style question. Not "what do I see," but "how did she do it, and why that way."

Check everything you can see on the surface:

Does the painting look finished or unfinished? Fast or slow? Careful or wild? Defend your answer with one detail.

The artist could have painted a photo-realistic deer, perfect in every hair. She painted a rough one instead. Which one would fit this story better, and why?

This painting is small — about the size of your two hands together. Does small make it feel more personal or less? Why?

A symbol is a real thing that stands for an idea. Fill in the last column. There is no single right answer, but you must be able to defend yours.

9 Now write

Give the painting a title. Then say why.

One sentence. What is this painting saying about the story?

Write one paragraph. Use these sentences to start. Change them if you want.

The first thing I notice is ______.
The artist uses ______ to show ______.
I think the deer stands for ______.
This connects to the story because ______.
When I look at this painting, I feel ______, because ______.

Last one. If the artist walked into this room right now, what is the one question you would ask her?

Teacher notes (not for students)

Why no King quotes are printed here

Both text hunts are deliberately unanswered on the page. Students must go back into the text to find the blueberry pail and the doe scene. If a group stalls, the hints narrow it without giving the line away.

The two passages students are hunting

  • Blueberries: Ray Brower went out picking berries with a pail and never came home — the detail that explains why a twelve-year-old was walking on the tracks at all. The MISSING poster on the wall also says he was carrying a bucket, so students can cross-check.
  • The doe: Gordie's dawn watch, alone, the doe on the tracks, and his decision not to tell the others. This is the emotional center of the whole novella and the reason the deer — not the boys — is the subject of this painting.

The reading the painting rewards

  • Top/bottom inversion: the heavy dark is above, pressing down; the ground is bleached pale. Sky as bruise.
  • The dominant color is the exact color of crushed berry and of dried blood. One image doing both jobs is the whole painting in one move.
  • The dots read as berries in the air, berries spilled from the pail, and as blood spatter, simultaneously.
  • Drips run over the deer's legs and across the physical edge of the canvas — the violence does not stay inside the frame.
  • Text collaged on the body, partly painted out: memory as something recovered in fragments. The story is called "The Body," and here the body is the surface the story is written on.

Running it

  • Sections 0–2 work as a whole-class warm-up with the real painting passed around or held up.
  • Sections 3–6 work well in pairs, one section per pair, then a gallery-style report back.
  • Section 8 (symbol chart) and 9 (paragraph) are the assessable pieces.
  • Answers save in the browser automatically on the same device and browser. Print gives a clean handout with all hints open and the toolbar hidden.
